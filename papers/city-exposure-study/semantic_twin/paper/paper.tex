\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An absolute radio-frequency exposure level in a city square depends on base
station powers, positions, and counts that are not public and are not properties
of the square. This paper measures an exposure ratio: power density at a
pedestrian's head in the square divided by that from the same network over open
ground. Reciprocity places the computation at the pedestrian, so one outward
trace represents every possible source position, and a photograph taken at that
same point sees the surfaces the early interactions reach. The method was
applied at 15\,GHz to 880
standpoints in eleven photogrammetric squares. Between-square spreads were
3.71, 4.93, and 9.58\,dB under isotropic, rooftop, and street-cell illumination.
Median within-square spreads were 3.92, 5.46, and 8.43\,dB. Those spreads
exceeded the between-square spread for the first two models but fell 1.15\,dB
short for street cells. The paired illumination-law correction had residual root mean square
values of 1.39\,dB rooftop and 0.98\,dB street, with one city per model causing
almost all rank change. Resampling the standpoints changed per-city medians by
0.128, 0.061, and 0.188\,dB root mean square, roughly 25 times the isotropic
Monte Carlo error. At the one square with registered panoramas, image-based
facade materials shifted distribution medians by 0.024, 0.029, and 0.022\,dB,
which bounds what image evidence can be worth on that axis. The other ten
squares carry no image evidence and use a geometric material assignment. A
geometry-specific diffraction bound
added median uplifts of 0.06, 0.15, and 0.42\,dB. The ratio therefore measures
the response of a square to a stated illumination law, with standpoint sampling
as its dominant quantified uncertainty.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Radio exposure in a city is usually computed from a known deployment. A ray
tracer receives a list of base stations, solves one transmitter-receiver problem
for each link, and adds the fields or powers. This construction is appropriate
when the deployment is known. It cannot separate the effect of the square from
the effect of the operator's confidential site count, power, and placement.
Those network variables leave the open-ground power density anywhere from 0.0075
to 3.0\,W/m$^2$ for rooftop sites and from 0.010 to 4.1\,W/m$^2$ for street
cells, a span of nearly three orders of magnitude. The general-public reference level at
15\,GHz is 10\,W/m$^2$~\cite{icnirp}. An absolute level therefore answers a
network question before it answers a city question.

The exposure ratio removes the unknown level. Its numerator is the power density
at the pedestrian's head in the real square. Its denominator is the power density
that the same network would give at the same point over open ground. Site power,
site density, and the absolute level divide out. The remaining number is one in
free space, less than one when buildings block more power than they return, and
greater than one when they return more than they block. The ratio asks how the square changes
exposure, which is the quantity that can be compared between cities.

The location of the computation changes what can be measured. Conventional
tracing starts at each transmitter. The present trace starts at the pedestrian,
follows rays outward, and weights each escaped ray by the probability that a
site lies in its exit direction. Reciprocity then reads every path backward.
The familiar benefit is computational, since one trace replaces a trace for
each possible source position. The larger benefit is evidential. A street-level
photograph taken at the same point sees the surfaces that the early ray
interactions reach. Material family can then be observed instead of assigned,
and the distance over which the photographs retain that coverage sets the
bounce budget.

Existing city-scale exposure studies solve careful per-pair problems over known
base-station positions~\cite{leeman}. Earlier work by the same author also joins
photogrammetric geometry, propagation, and anatomical dosimetry for specified
deployments~\cite{wydaeghe2026}. The question here is different. No site list is
available, and the output must remain useful when a deployment model changes.
The point-centered ratio is therefore a physical separation between the
environment and the network, rather than a faster implementation of the same
per-pair calculation.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it applies a reciprocal outward
estimator to a continuous site distribution and preserves the arriving angular
power at the pedestrian. Second, it measures how much of each early interaction
lands on geometry visible from the same viewpoint, and uses that to set the
bounce budget from image coverage rather than from a convergence tolerance. That
second contribution is the one with no precedent found. Third, it compares the
resulting ratio at 880 standpoints in eleven squares under three illumination
laws after measuring the crop radius. Fourth, it tests the claim against material
discrimination, matched and steered beams, bystanders, truncation, polarization,
and diffraction.

The individual ingredients are older than this paper and are attributed as such
in Section~\ref{sec:related}. Reciprocal sensor-side transport, stochastic
deployment integration, image-derived materials, and multi-city exposure
comparison are all established. What is offered here is their combination for a
deployment-averaged ratio, and the image-coverage budget that is available only
once the camera and the ray source share a point.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
positions the estimator against per-pair tracing, deployment integration, image
materials, and adjoint transport. Sections~\ref{sec:ratio}--\ref{sec:valid}
define and check the method. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports six
results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them and states every known bias
with its sign. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Deterministic radio ray tracers solve paths between named endpoints. Sionna RT,
for example, computes propagation paths between transmitter and receiver
antennas~\cite{sionna}. Leeman \emph{et al.} use that per-pair construction for
city-scale downlink exposure with base-station positions taken from public
conformity records~\cite{leeman}. The approach is physically detailed and
auditable. It gives path delay, phase, and diffraction for the deployment that
was supplied. Its cost and its output remain tied to that deployment, so a new
site layout requires a new set of link calculations. The present work replaces
that discrete input with a continuous angular distribution because no
deployment is known.

Stochastic deployment models already connect spatial site distributions to
exposure. Wiame \emph{et al.} compare stochastic geometry and deterministic ray
tracing in a Manhattan environment~\cite{wiame}. The LEXNET exposure index
precomputes normalized body coefficients and combines them later with a
deployment-dependent incident level~\cite{varsier}. These are close precedents,
not omissions in earlier work. The distinction here is that the environment and
the deployment remain separate before the body calculation. The square gives
an angular response, the possible sites give an angular illumination, and the
body receives their product. A scalar environment-and-deployment term cannot be
reweighted after it has discarded that angle.

Comparisons between cities are also established. Veludo \emph{et al.} measured
radio-frequency fields across microenvironments in ten European countries
\cite{veludo}. The earlier photogrammetric study by Wydaeghe \emph{et al.}
computed absorbed power density for specified outdoor deployments in more than
one city~\cite{wydaeghe2026}. The present contribution is therefore not the act
of comparing cities. What it compares is the property left after the same
network is divided out, at a frequency and angular resolution for which the
unknown deployment would otherwise dominate the result.

Image-derived radio materials are published as well. The mmSV pipeline segments
street-view images into material classes, projects them onto city geometry, and
uses them in millimeter-wave ray tracing~\cite{mmsv}. The present paper does not
claim that projection as new. Here the camera and the ray source share a
viewpoint. Co-location turns image coverage into a statement about which
surfaces the early interactions hit. The image is therefore used to bound what
the propagation model knows, rather than only to populate a material table.

Adjoint transport predates radio ray tracing. Veach gives the sensor-side
importance formulation used in rendering~\cite{veach}. Daylight coefficients
precompute a response to a discretized sky~\cite{tregenza}, and precomputed
radiance transfer stores a point response that can be combined with changing
illumination~\cite{sloan}. These works establish reciprocity, sensor-side
tracing, and source substitution. The contribution here is their use for a
deployment-averaged exposure ratio at a pedestrian, together with a measured
image-coverage budget and body coupling. The individual mathematical ingredients
are prior art.

One limit on this survey is worth stating rather than leaving for a reader to
discover. The survey rests on title and abstract indexing across four databases
plus direct reading of the sources cited above, and its coverage of 2023 to 2026
preprints is thinner than its coverage of the journal record. A recent paper
building the same reciprocal construction for radio would not necessarily have
surfaced. The claims above are therefore claims about what was found, not
proofs that nothing else exists.

\subsection{Eleven squares}

The square changed the exposure ratio by several decibels, but one number per
square lost much of the variation. \Cref{tab:result-summary} compares the 5th to
95th percentile spread inside each square with the spread of the eleven square
medians. The median within-square spread was 3.92\,dB isotropic and 5.46\,dB
rooftop, above the corresponding between-square spreads of 3.71 and 4.93\,dB.
Six and eight of the eleven squares exceeded those between-square values. The
street-cell model was the counter case. Its median within-square spread was
8.43\,dB against 9.58\,dB between squares, and 4 of 11 squares exceeded the
between-square value. A single number per square is therefore a poor summary of
that square under most of the illumination models considered, though not under
all of them. Where a pedestrian stands matters more than which of these eleven
squares they stand in.

\Cref{fig:eleven-exposure} gives the distributions for the corrected rooftop
law, the sky fraction, and the corresponding body-side peak absorbed power
density. The same geometric material prior is used everywhere. Mexico City's
Zocalo had the largest sampled median. The fixed ground datum removes the roof
placements that had made Krakow and Toulouse outliers in an earlier run. The
correction changed Krakow by $-1.40$\,dB isotropic and $-12.97$\,dB street, and
changed Toulouse by $-1.29$ and $-2.61$\,dB. Every other square changed by less
than 0.25\,dB under isotropic illumination. Rooftop and street rankings have
Spearman correlations of $+0.936$ and $+0.345$ with the isotropic ranking.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives the standpoint-sampling uncertainty that
qualifies adjacent per-city medians. This ordering is an ordering under the
illumination law with no antenna. The beamforming result below shows that a
realistic antenna policy reverses pairs more than a decibel apart, so the
ordering should be read together with that result rather than on its own.

\subsection{Illumination changes the order}

The corrected illumination law changed levels and city order on identical rays.
At the 250\,m Korenmarkt crop, the correction increased the rooftop ratio by
5.67\,dB and the street ratio by 3.98\,dB. The rooftop change exceeded the
4.93\,dB spread between all eleven squares. Since both laws used the same rays,
their differential Monte Carlo noise was exactly zero.

The eleven-city shifts were not a common change of level. Rooftop shifts ran from
$+1.05$ to $+6.33$\,dB about a mean of $+5.14$\,dB, with a residual root mean
square of 1.39\,dB. Street shifts ran from $+1.45$ to $+4.41$\,dB about a mean
of $+3.43$\,dB, with a residual root mean square of 0.98\,dB. The earlier and
corrected rankings had Spearman correlations of $+0.65$ rooftop and $+0.88$
street. Nine sites changed rank rooftop and eight changed rank street, but one
site per model caused almost all of the reordering. New York fell from first to
ninth rooftop, and Tokyo fell from fourth to eighth street. Removing that one
site gave a Spearman correlation of $+0.98$ in both models, with no remaining
site moving more than one place.

The earlier law concentrated power immediately above its lower elevation limit.
Across the eleven squares, the share of the earlier rooftop answer arriving below
$5^\circ$ predicted the residual about the mean correction with Pearson
correlation $-0.97$. New York left narrow near-horizontal channels between its
towers, which is where the earlier law put most of its weight. The corrected law
is therefore close to a
level shift for nine or ten squares, but it is not one at the top of the ranking.

\subsection{Material discrimination bounds the claim}

Two image-based material compositions that assign visibly different facade
classes give the same exposure distribution. The two comparisons here and the
bracket at the end of the subsection ran on the three configurations
\cref{tab:configs} gives for them, none of which is the headline one.
Replacing the entity-based facade
prior with the image-derived material classes changes the distribution median by
0.024\,dB under isotropic illumination, 0.029\,dB under rooftop illumination,
and 0.022\,dB under street-cell illumination. The within-Korenmarkt
spreads of that 120-standpoint population were 3.9, 8.4, and 16.7\,dB, which are
not the 2.85, 6.39, and 8.43\,dB of the Korenmarkt row of
\cref{tab:result-summary} and are a different population rather than a
disagreement. The 0.024\,dB isotropic shift was 5.7 times
the 0.0042\,dB standard error of the walk median, so the design resolved the
shift rather than missing it, and the shift is a small part of one decibel.

Changing the whole material field gave a larger result with the opposite sign.
The isotropic median changed by $-0.218$\,dB, 15 of 120 standpoints exceeded
1\,dB, and the worst changed by 2.020\,dB. Rooftop and street medians changed by
$-0.156$ and $-0.024$\,dB. The ground changed in this comparison as well as the
facades, so the result assigns more material sensitivity to the pavement than to
facade discrimination.

A paired 24-standpoint bracket fixed every facade to one material. Render and
stone changed the isotropic ratio by $-0.104$ and $+0.239$\,dB, and changed the
rooftop ratio by $-0.164$ and $+0.414$\,dB. The dielectric span of the medians
was therefore
0.34\,dB isotropic and 0.58\,dB rooftop, while the worst single standpoints in
that bracket reached $+1.56$\,dB rooftop, $+2.48$\,dB street and $-2.67$\,dB
street. Metal changed the same medians by
$+2.502$ and $+4.374$\,dB, with all 24 standpoints above 1\,dB. The measured
bracket separates uncertainty within masonry from a change of material family.

The null result licenses the geometric assignment only within the observed
material family. The photographs show that the reference square is masonry.
They do not estimate a finely tuned permittivity. A square dominated by metal,
or by glazing whose layer stack is unknown, remains outside the tested family
because the mesh alone does not identify that change. Material discrimination
therefore sets the boundary of the cross-city claim: the reported comparison
applies to masonry squares.

\subsection{Beamforming depends on what the beam follows}

Full digital maximum ratio transmission cancels from the ratio exactly. The
matched filter collects the total channel power, and the array gain multiplies
both the real-square numerator and the open-ground denominator. The result needs
no assumption about how many paths reach the pedestrian or which directions
they occupy. The element pattern remains and changes the weighting by 0.26\,dB
at the rooftop median elevation of $9.5^\circ$ and by 10.2\,dB at the
$60^\circ$ top of the band.

Geometric steering did not share the exact cancellation. A reflected path leaves
the site toward its final surface, whereas a coordinate-steering beam points
toward the head. A retrace at 20 Korenmarkt standpoints recorded the exit
direction and last surface for each path. \Cref{tab:steering} gives the median
change relative to the reported ratio. For an $8\times8$ panel, geometric
steering changed the rooftop ratio by $-0.38$\,dB, with 10th and 90th percentiles
of $-0.49$ and $-0.25$\,dB. The street change was $-1.64$\,dB, with percentiles
of $-2.53$ and $-1.09$\,dB.

The direct part fixed the lower bound. It was 0.6955 of the rooftop ratio and
0.5054 of the street ratio, so no beam aimed at the pedestrian could reduce the
two ratios by more than 1.58 and 2.96\,dB. The $32\times32$ street result reached
96\% of that floor.

A codebook is the same axis rather than a second one. Selecting the nearest beam
from a grid at least as fine as the aperture changed the rooftop and street
ratios by $-0.25$ and $-1.54$\,dB at 8 beams per axis, $-0.37$ and $-1.61$\,dB at
16, and $-0.37$ and $-1.66$\,dB at 32, converging on the exact steering values of
$-0.38$ and $-1.64$\,dB. Codebook granularity is therefore worth under 0.15\,dB
and is not one of the parameters that sets the range. Grids coarser than the
aperture were computed and excluded, because the nearest grid beam then sits more
than a beamwidth from the target and the free-space reference lands in a pattern
null, which returns $+50$\,dB. No scheduler picks a beam that nulls its own user.
A real network with a coarse beam set broadens its beams instead, and a broadened
beam is a smaller effective aperture, which is the aperture table read upward.

The remaining gap is best-beam selection, and the inequality has to be stated
carefully because the loose version is false. Exact steering is not a member of a
finite codebook, so it is not ordered against best-beam selection at all. What is
ordered is nearest-beam against best-beam on the \emph{same} grid. Best-beam
maximises the sum that nearest-beam evaluates at one member, and in free space
with a single path both pick the same beam, so the two references are equal and
the best-beam ratio is at least the nearest-beam ratio. The measured column is
therefore a lower bound on the best-beam ratio rather than an estimate of it.
Where best-beam actually sits needs a finite-site trace, because the stored
estimator does not retain which paths belong to one site.

One caution about the element pattern, because the taper value invites the wrong
arithmetic. Averaged over the rooftop population under its own measure the
element is 0.45\,dB down, but its measured effect on the ratio is 0.62\,dB at the
median square and 1.07\,dB at Madrid. The element down-weights steep arrivals,
and steep is where a square lets illumination through, so it costs two to four
times what the taper at the median elevation suggests. The 0.26\,dB figure above
is a taper at one elevation and not a change in the ratio. The element cancels
only if its own boresight tracks the pedestrian, which needs mechanical pointing
in three axes. Deployed macro panels are mechanically fixed and steer
electronically, so under every policy a network actually runs the element
survives.

Realistic antenna policies also move the eleven-square order, by more than
adjacent swaps. Against the reported ordering, Kendall correlations are 0.818 for
a rooftop broadcast beam and 0.709 for a loaded street beam, reversing pairs up
to 1.53\,dB apart rooftop and 2.97\,dB street on axes 5.6 and 9.8\,dB wide. Full
digital maximum ratio transmission is the one policy that nearly preserves the
order, reversing no street pair and two rooftop pairs at 0.58\,dB or less. The
ordering reported here is therefore an ordering under the stated illumination law
with no antenna, and it is not invariant to what the network points.

\subsection{Bystanders alter the arriving distribution}

Geometry predicts strong elevation selectivity for a crowd. A standing adult
adds 0.23\,m above the head. A ray clears that height after 13\,m at
$1^\circ$, 2.6\,m at $5^\circ$, and 0.4\,m at $30^\circ$. Since the street and
rooftop models send 88\% and 9\% of their measure below $5^\circ$, the geometric
prediction was a factor of nine between their crowd losses.

The trace rejects that prediction. At 2.15 people per square meter, represented
by 2867 bodies, the street model lost 1.40\,dB and the rooftop model lost
1.07\,dB. The ratio of losses was
1.3 instead of nine. The sent angular measure is not the arriving measure. The
arriving shares below $5^\circ$ are 0.212 and 0.185, a ratio of 1.15, because the
facades redistribute the power before it reaches the crowd. The isotropic model
changed by only 0.070\,dB. Its paired noise floor was 0.009\,dB, so the isotropic
change was eight times the floor rather than absent, while the rooftop and street
noise floors of 0.035 and 0.112\,dB put their changes 30 and 12 times above it.
What straddles zero is the spread across standpoints, whose isotropic 5th and
95th percentiles are $-0.59$ and $+0.04$\,dB.

The absorber control separated blockage from reflection. Replacing the bodies
with index-matched absorbers changed the isotropic loss from 0.070 to 0.708\,dB,
whereas the rooftop loss changed from 1.069 to 1.332\,dB and the street loss from
1.396 to 1.607\,dB. The bodies therefore became almost invisible to isotropic
illumination because they reflected blocked power into other directions. Every
number in this subsection was traced at the reported three-interaction budget
with roulette off. The crowd arm was retraced at that budget on the same mesh,
the same measured datum and the same 12 standpoints, and these paired shifts
moved by at most 0.03\,dB from the earlier six-interaction run.

\subsection{Crop radius is measured}

The crop radius follows from a convergence trace rather than a visual choice.
\Cref{fig:crop-convergence} holds the observers fixed and changes only the
surrounding geometry. Convergence meant the smallest radius from which every
later step stayed below 0.5\,dB. Isotropic illumination and sky fraction met the
criterion at 60\,m, rooftop illumination at 100\,m, and street illumination at
200\,m. The common 250\,m operating point was therefore set by the street model
alone and lay one crop step past the criterion. Increasing the crop from 130 to
250\,m changed the isotropic, rooftop, and street ratios by $-0.06$, $-0.56$,
and $-7.05$\,dB.

The deployment range cap was different and did not converge. Sweeping it from
100 to 400\,m changed the median absolute ratio by 7.40\,dB rooftop and 9.24\,dB
street. Between-city contrast changed by 0.51\,dB at the median, but the ranking
was not invariant: its endpoint Spearman correlation was 0.81 and 7 of 11 sites
changed rank. The ratio must therefore be quoted with its deployment cap even
after the mesh crop has converged.

\section{Discussion and threats to validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results separate two sources of variability that an absolute exposure level
mixes together. The network sets the open-ground level. The square and the
illumination law set the ratio. The law correction was close to a common shift
for most squares, but New York and Tokyo showed that it could change the top of
the ranking. The ratio is therefore a response of a mesh to a stated source
distribution. Another deployment hypothesis can be applied without rebuilding
the square or choosing discrete transmitters, but it produces a different
comparison.

Standpoint sampling limits the resolution of that comparison. A change of less
than 0.25\,m in the ground datum replaced 56 to 76 of the 80 sampled standpoints
at eight sites because the candidate chain was reordered. The resulting per-city
medians changed by 0.128, 0.061, and 0.188\,dB root mean square under isotropic,
rooftop, and street illumination, as recorded when the datum was changed. Two of
the eleven pre-change row files are torn on disk, leaving 78 valid rows at
Times Square and 80 at Prague from a file carrying one unparseable extra line,
so these three figures rest on 878 of the 880 pre-change standpoints and
recomputing from what remains gives 0.125, 0.066, and 0.181\,dB. The reported
run itself parses end to end at 880 rows. The largest changes were
0.242, 0.116, and 0.326\,dB. These values are 7 to 47 times their Monte Carlo floors. The
3.71--9.58\,dB headline spreads remain resolved, but differences between adjacent
city medians of a few tenths of a decibel do not.

The computation at the person is more than an amortization.
\Cref{fig:evidence-reach} separates geometric visibility from photographic
coverage. Closed paths stayed on visible surfaces with shares 1.000000,
0.999972, and 0.980 over their first three interactions. Outward paths retained
0.999103, 0.912004, and 0.714182. At a camera position, 7 of 8 registered
stations covered 0.978 to 0.999 of the first interaction. The second interaction
retained 93\% to 96\% coverage within 20\,m, whereas every depth fell below
0.21 beyond 40\,m. First-interaction coverage over the published 90\,m walk was
0.479. The image evidence is therefore local to the instrument.

Three interactions remain sufficient on both evidence and power grounds.
\Cref{fig:bounce-budget} shows that 21\% of launched power escaped directly,
whereas 79\%, 8.8\%, and 1.2\% reached the first three surfaces, these being the
power reaching each depth and not a partition to be summed. Interactions
past the third carried 0.23\% of launched power. Those shares and the two that
follow were measured at the reference square on a 130\,m crop. At the reported
250\,m crop they become 23.3\% direct and 76.7\%, 8.7\%, and 1.0\%. Against an
eight-interaction
reference, the three-interaction budget changed the median by 0.002\,dB and no
standpoint by more than 0.063\,dB over the 40 standpoints of the published walk,
and by no more than 0.384\,dB over four squares at the 250\,m crop. The
truncated share was 0.0037 of escaping
power at the median of those 40 standpoints and 0.0201 at the worst, and 0.0050
and 0.0795 over the 880 standpoints reported here. Roulette changed neither
seed spread nor run time at this budget, so it was switched off.

A co-located return does not replace the outward estimator. Across 440
standpoints, 98.6\% of the return is first order and its power-weighted two-way
range is 4.05\,m. Its median is $-70.81$\,dB against $-70.99$\,dB for a rough
half-space calculation. The instrument mostly sees its own pavement. The pooled
Spearman correlation with the exposure ratio is $-0.526$, but the partial
correlation at fixed sky fraction is $-0.010$. Sky fraction alone correlates at
$+0.961$. The second-order return retains a partial correlation of $-0.345$
with the reflected ratio, but it is 0.37\% of received power and sits 24\,dB
below the pavement return. The closed-loop geometry gives the image-visibility
guarantee. Its return amplitude is not a practical proxy for the exposure ratio.

The material result is a bound on generalization. Masonry facade substitutions
changed the median by hundredths of a decibel, and the full dielectric bracket
spanned 0.34\,dB isotropic and 0.58\,dB rooftop. Metal changed those medians by
2.502 and 4.374\,dB. The larger whole-field change also showed that the pavement
deserves more material effort than small distinctions among facades. Applying
the geometric facade default to a metal-clad square would change material family
rather than refine a masonry value. The eleven-square comparison therefore
applies to the observed masonry family.

Diffraction is small for two models and not for the third.
\Cref{fig:diffraction-bound} gives medians of 0.06, 0.15, and 0.42\,dB, with
90th percentiles of 0.13, 0.37, and 1.26\,dB. The street percentile uses the 56
standpoints with a defined uplift. Four more had their entire street elevation
support occluded, so the direct term was zero. The largest defined street bound
was 12.64\,dB, at a standpoint whose traced ratio was among the smallest in the
study. The omission remains a bounded correction under isotropic and rooftop
illumination, but not at every street-cell standpoint.

Sionna RT, an independently written tracer, checked the reflected term on the
same meshes and materials. The worst of the three illumination models differed by
0.25\,dB at the reference square and 0.16\,dB at the Grand-Place, each a median
over standpoints, and the median across the three models was 0.24 and
0.03\,dB. The Monte Carlo floor on the comparison was 0.23 to 0.35\,dB. No
disagreement was resolved. The check tests transport but not the illumination law because both
calculations use the same $Q$.

\Cref{tab:honesty} states the known errors and scope limits with their sizes and
directions. Several errors point low. Diffraction adds paths, facade polarization
averaging loses up to 3.0\,dB, and truncation deletes power. The
ground-polarization error points high near $24^\circ$ elevation and can reach
tens of decibels on the affected paths. Standpoint sampling, body self-shadow,
and diffuse-scattering strength have unknown signs. Diffuse scattering is the
larger physical uncertainty. Published ablations move
prediction error from 6--13\,dB to 25--37\,dB at 28 and 38\,GHz when diffuse
scattering is removed~\cite{vitucci}.

Monte Carlo error is smaller than standpoint sampling. Measured over eight
disjoint seed streams, the standard errors of a
walk median are 0.0042\,dB isotropic, 0.0136\,dB rooftop, and 0.0343\,dB street.
The per-seed medians behind those three figures were not retained, so the
standard errors are quoted from the record of that measurement rather than
recomputed from stored output.
An older street-cell value of 0.079\,dB was the lowest of eight seed draws. The seed-averaged shift is $0.167 \pm 0.022$\,dB, or 7.6 standard
errors on the mean of eight and 2.7 on one run. The earlier 0.298\,dB headline
used a superseded illumination law and was never measured under the corrected
law. Neither superseded value is used as a result here.

The bystander experiment adds a separate scope limit. It uses one masonry square
at one carrier with static upright people. It has been repeated at the reported
three-interaction budget on the same 12 standpoints. The rooftop and street
headline numbers moved by less than the estimator's own noise floor for their
columns, by 0.025\,dB against 0.035\,dB and by 0.005\,dB against 0.112\,dB. The
isotropic one moved 0.021\,dB against a floor of 0.009\,dB, so the column with
the smallest shift is also the one whose retrace is resolvable, and it is the
column the mechanism argument turns on. Its mechanism
is still informative: the facades had already changed the arriving elevation
distribution before a crowd blocked it. The 1.4 and 1.1\,dB shifts are paired
Korenmarkt results rather than city-independent corrections.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study showed that the shape of a city square changes relative pedestrian
exposure at 15\,GHz by several decibels after the unknown network level is
divided out. The illumination law changed the city order, so the result belongs
to the pair formed by the square and the assumed site distribution. Variation
inside a typical square exceeded the variation between square medians under
isotropic and rooftop illumination, but not under street-cell illumination.
Standpoint sampling also exceeded Monte Carlo error. A city value therefore
needs a distribution of pedestrian standpoints and an uncertainty from sampling
those points.

Computing at the pedestrian joined propagation to observable evidence. A
co-located photograph covered the surfaces that received the early interactions,
and the measured decay of that coverage set the bounce budget. Material
discrimination inside the observed masonry family then changed the exposure
ratio by only hundredths of a decibel. The measured dielectric bracket spanned
0.58\,dB at the rooftop median and 1.56\,dB at its worst standpoint, whereas
metal changed the rooftop median by 4.374\,dB. Geometry
carries the comparison among masonry squares, and image evidence is needed to
determine whether a new square belongs to that family.

Future work must trace finite sites to close the gap between nearest-beam and
best-beam selection, and must fit diffuse scattering rather than model it, since
that is the largest remaining physical uncertainty. A stable spatial sampling
rule would remove the dominant numerical one, which more rays cannot. The
bystander study also needs repetition beyond one square.
