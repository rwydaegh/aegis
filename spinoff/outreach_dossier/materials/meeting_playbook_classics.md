# The classic company-meeting playbook (a short tutorial)

*Claude Opus, May 2026. The generic, well-worn tips-and-tricks that any company uses for discovery, sales, and partnership meetings, with the named frameworks so you can read further. AEGIS-specific application is in `company_outreach_and_ndas.md`. This one is the general theory.*

---

## The point of a meeting (read this first)

The single biggest beginner mistake is thinking a meeting exists to *close*. It almost never does. A first meeting exists to do four things, in this order of usefulness:

1. **Learn.** What is their actual problem, workflow, and budget reality. Most of your assumptions are wrong and the meeting is how you find out cheaply.
2. **Qualify.** Is this even a real opportunity. Are you talking to someone who has the problem, the authority, and the money. Disqualifying fast is a win, not a loss.
3. **Build trust.** People buy from and partner with people they believe are competent and honest. One meeting rarely builds enough. Several do.
4. **Advance.** End with a concrete next step that moves things forward.

Neil Rackham (SPIN Selling) drew the key distinction: a good meeting ends in an **advance** (a specific action with a date: a technical eval, an intro to the decision-maker, a pilot scope) versus a **continuation** ("great chat, let's stay in touch"), which feels nice and means nothing. If your meeting ends without a dated next action, treat it as a soft no.

## The named frameworks worth knowing

You do not need to read all of these. Knowing they exist tells you the field is solved and you can borrow from it.

- **The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)** — the bible for discovery / learning meetings. One idea: talk about *their* life, not *your* idea.
- **Customer Development (Steve Blank) / Lean Startup (Eric Ries)** — "get out of the building." A startup is a search for a repeatable business, and meetings are the search.
- **SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham)** — for larger, considered purchases. Ask, don't pitch. The good seller talks *less* than the buyer.
- **The Challenger Sale (Dixon & Adamson)** — teach the customer something new about their own problem, tailor it to them, take control of the conversation. Works when you know the domain better than they do (you do).
- **Getting to Yes (Fisher & Ury)** and **Never Split the Difference (Chris Voss)** — negotiation, for the partnership / pricing stage.
- **BANT / MEDDIC** — qualification checklists (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline / Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, etc). Useful to know *who* you're actually talking to.

## Before the meeting

- **Set one goal.** What is the single advance you want. "Get them to agree to a technical eval" or "understand their compliance workflow" or "get a yes to a letter of support." One. Not five.
- **Research them.** Their product, their role, recent news, who their customers are. Ten minutes of homework changes how seriously you're taken.
- **Send a short agenda in advance.** Even two lines. "30 min: quick demo, then I mostly want to understand how X works on your side, then next steps." This is the Sandler "up-front contract": agree what the meeting is for and how it ends *before* it starts, so nobody is surprised and you can ask for the next step without it being awkward.
- **Pick the right person.** A meeting with someone who can't decide and doesn't own the problem is pleasant and useless. Better an intro to the right person than a long chat with the wrong one.

---

## During the meeting

- **Talk less than you think.** Conversation-analytics studies of thousands of recorded B2B calls (Gong, Chorus) consistently find the same thing: in *winning* discovery calls the seller talks under half the time and the buyer does most of the talking. In losing ones the seller monologues. Aim to listen 60-70% of the time.
- **Open with credibility, briefly.** Who you are and why you're worth their time, in two sentences. Then move off yourself.
- **Demo with discipline.** If you show a product, show it fast and let it speak. Do not narrate every feature. The demo is bait for *their* reaction, not a lecture.
- **Ask about the past, not the future.** This is the core Mom Test move. "How do you handle this today?" and "When did you last hit this problem, what did you do?" give real data. "Would you use a tool that does X?" gives polite lies. People are terrible at predicting their own future behavior and great at describing what they actually did last month.
- **Dig into problems before solutions.** SPIN's sequence: ask about their situation, surface the problem, draw out its *implications* (what it costs them), and only then connect to what you offer. A problem they haven't felt the cost of is not a problem they'll pay to fix.
- **Let them say no.** Voss's point: "no" makes people feel safe and in control, and it gives you real information. A meeting where everything is "yes, interesting, great" usually produced nothing true.
- **Watch for fake positives.** Compliments, vague enthusiasm, and "I'd definitely buy that" are the three worst signals in the room. They feel like progress and are noise. Real signals cost the other person something: their time (a follow-up booked), their reputation (an intro to their boss), or their money.
- **Always end with the next step.** Propose it explicitly. "Shall we book 45 minutes with your technical lead?" Silence after a good meeting is where deals die.

## After the meeting

- **Follow up the same day.** Short. Thank them, summarize what you heard (shows you listened, corrects misunderstandings), restate the agreed next step with a date.
- **Write down what you learned** while it's fresh, especially the exact words they used for their problem. Those words become your next pitch.
- **Do the thing you promised, fast.** The cheapest trust-builder in business is doing the small follow-up thing you said you'd do, on time.

## The discovery-meeting special case (when the goal is to learn, not sell)

If the meeting's purpose is learning (early customer-discovery calls), invert everything:

- You are not there to convince them. You are there to be educated.
- Never pitch. The moment you pitch, they switch from honest-informant mode to polite-or-defensive mode and the data goes bad.
- Ask, listen, ask why, shut up. Silence is a tool. People fill it with the truth.
- Leave with facts about their world, not flattery about your idea.

This is the cheapest, highest-value meeting type and the one founders avoid most, because it has no dopamine hit. Do it anyway.

## The partnership / negotiation special case

When the meeting is about a deal between two companies (not a simple sale):

- **Interests, not positions (Getting to Yes).** Don't argue over a number. Find what each side actually needs. A partner who wants distribution and a partner who wants validation can both win without fighting over price.
- **Know your BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement). Your walk-away power comes from having other options. If this is your only path, they know it.
- **Make the other side's success part of the framing.** The strongest partnership pitch is "this makes *your* thing more valuable," not "this is good for me." (For a fast-pre-screener-on-top-of-FDTD story, that's "more pre-screens means more rigorous full-wave runs on the worst cases," not "I'll take your customers.")

## The cardinal sins (what kills these meetings)

1. Pitching when you should be listening.
2. Treating compliments and "sounds great" as progress.
3. Talking to someone with no authority and no budget and calling it a pipeline.
4. Ending with no concrete, dated next step.
5. Asking hypothetical future questions instead of past-behavior questions.
6. Over-claiming. One exaggeration the expert catches and you lose the room.
7. Over-lawyering early. A heavy NDA or contract demand on a first friendly call kills momentum. Keep early steps light and let the legal weight come when there's real substance to protect.

---

## TL;DR

A meeting is for learning, qualifying, trust, and advancing, not for closing. Research them, set one goal, send a two-line agenda. Talk less than half the time, ask about their past not your future, dig into the cost of their problem before offering anything, and treat compliments as noise. End every meeting with a dated next step, follow up the same day, and do what you promised. For learning calls, never pitch. For partnerships, sell their upside, not yours.
