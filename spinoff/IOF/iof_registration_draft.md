## On using "AEGIS" in the title

The official expansion is *Adaptive Electromagnetic Geometric Illumination & Safety*. The user suggested "AEGIS: Adaptive Electromagnetic Geometric Illumination & Safety" — i.e. just the name followed by what it stands for. That works and has no awkwardness. The downside is that the expansion itself is opaque to a non-expert TT reviewer: "Illumination" sounds like lighting, "Geometric" is abstract, and nothing in the expansion tells you it's about 5G compliance or that it replaces a simulation that used to take hours.

That said, the 4 sentences do the explaining, so the title doesn't have to carry the full value proposition. A clean, identifiable name is enough for a registration form.

## Title brainstorm

### Approach 1: just the name and expansion (user's suggestion)
> AEGIS: Adaptive Electromagnetic Geometric Illumination & Safety

**Pros:** official, no redundancy, name will be consistent across IDF/IOF/patent/paper. Clean.
**Cons:** opaque to TT staff — nothing in the expansion signals 5G, compliance, or speed. "Illumination" is misleading (sounds like optics). Works best if they'll hear the name often; weak as a one-shot title on a form.

### Approach 2: AEGIS as brand + punchy subtitle (dash, not colon)
> AEGIS — Millisecond Electromagnetic Exposure Assessment for 5G/6G Compliance

**Pros:** "milliseconds" is the single most powerful fact about the project — TT staff will immediately understand the breakthrough. 5G/6G compliance is a hot, recognized market. The dash signals "brand name — what it does" without implying the subtitle expands the acronym.
**Cons:** subtitle is a bit long; "millisecond" as an adjective is unusual phrasing.

### Approach 3: brand + method name
> AEGIS — Geometric Dosimetry for 5G/6G Electromagnetic Exposure Compliance

**Pros:** "geometric dosimetry" is the IP/method name — consistent with the IDF, which is important since the IOF reviewer will eventually cross-reference both. Names the thing, not just a capability.
**Cons:** "dosimetry" is jargon (though TT in this domain will know it).

### Approach 4: brand + outcome
> AEGIS — Real-Time Electromagnetic Safety Compliance for 5G/6G Networks

**Pros:** "real-time" is accessible; "safety compliance" is plain language. Closest to how you'd describe it to a non-technical investor.
**Cons:** "real-time" is somewhat weaker than "milliseconds" — it's a promise not a number. Doesn't signal the physics breakthrough.

### Approach 5: descriptive, no brand
> Geometric Dosimetry: Closed-Form Electromagnetic Exposure Assessment for 5G/6G

**Pros:** names the method (which is the IP). "Closed-form" is a meaningful signal to anyone technical — it means no simulation, mathematical derivation. Works well if AEGIS as a brand isn't established with this audience.
**Cons:** no brand recognition; "geometric dosimetry" and "closed-form" may both need explaining.

### Approach 6: market-forward, no brand
> Real-Time Human Exposure Compliance Software for 5G/6G Network Planning and Device Design

**Pros:** completely plain language, no jargon, covers both markets (operators + OEMs).
**Cons:** generic — could describe a dozen products. No signal of the underlying novelty. Weak for an IP-heavy registration.

---

## Verdict

**Winner: Approach 2 — `AEGIS — Millisecond Electromagnetic Exposure Assessment for 5G/6G Compliance`**

Reasoning: the registration form is read by TT staff who assess 20+ projects per call. The single fact that will make them lean forward is "milliseconds vs hours of simulation." That concrete number does more work than any amount of correct-but-abstract terminology ("geometric," "closed-form," "Fresnel"). Keeping AEGIS in front establishes the brand early, which matters for continuity across IDF, IOF, and eventual patents. The dash reads as "product name — what it does," avoiding the acronym-expansion confusion.

**Runner-up: Approach 1** (`AEGIS: Adaptive Electromagnetic Geometric Illumination & Safety`) if you want to keep it minimal and let the 4 sentences carry all the content. Perfectly clean; just less punchy.

**Skip approaches 5–6**: the form is an opportunity to establish the brand. No reason to drop AEGIS here.

## 4-sentence description — three variants

### Variant 1 — balanced (recommended)
Certifying 5G/6G antennas against human exposure limits requires FDTD simulation, which takes hours per configuration and cannot scale to network planning or iterative device pre-screening.
AEGIS replaces volumetric simulation with a closed-form surface absorption law derived from Fresnel theory, computing a full body exposure map in milliseconds with less than 5% error across 6–100 GHz.
The platform connects to real antenna databases, reconstructs 3D urban environments from open map data, and supports gradient-based optimisation of antenna placement and MIMO beamforming under ICNIRP limits — no commercial tool offers this combination.
Target customers are mobile network operators (site-specific compliance), wireless chipset OEMs (handset pre-screening without measurement hardware), and regulatory consultancies, a market growing with 5G densification and 6G standardisation.

### Variant 2 — punchy, less technical
Demonstrating ICNIRP compliance for 5G/6G antennas currently requires hours of full-wave numerical simulation per configuration, blocking real-time network planning and fast device design.
AEGIS uses a physics-derived closed-form law to compute human body exposure maps in milliseconds, replacing the simulation with a surface-geometry calculation accurate to within 5% of the gold standard.
A web viewer pulls live base station data from 14 European government databases, reconstructs city geometry from open map data, and lets operators check compliance and optimise antenna parameters interactively.
The primary markets are network operators, handset chipset manufacturers screening designs before certification, and test laboratories — all facing growing demand as 5G densifies and 6G standardisation accelerates.

### Variant 3 — shortest, Dutch-register friendly
5G/6G network operators must prove ICNIRP compliance per antenna site, but standard simulation (FDTD) takes hours per run — too slow for interactive planning or device-level pre-screening.
The engine computes the same regulatory metric on a realistic 3D human body in milliseconds, using a closed-form physical model derived from Fresnel electromagnetic theory (< 5% error, 6–100 GHz).
A web-based viewer integrates real antenna data from European databases, reconstructs urban environments automatically, and optimises antenna placement and beamforming for compliance — capabilities absent from all current commercial tools.
Target markets are network operators, wireless chipset OEMs, and regulatory test labs; demand grows with 5G densification and the 6G standardisation cycle.
