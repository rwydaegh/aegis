\begin{abstract}
We extend the AEGIS surface dosimetry framework to the near field of a
hand-held device and use it to answer two questions raised by the dose-model
team: how normalized SAR depends on the device-to-body distance, and what
uncertainty should accompany each normalized value. The radiating element
(an IFA/PIFA on a smartphone) is electrically small, so its Fraunhofer distance
is a few centimetres and the field at the body is locally plane at every
surface point, with a per-triangle direction and amplitude fixed by the
measured free-space directivity and the spherical spreading. From this we
recover the full set of ICNIRP~2020 metrics, whole-body SAR, $\psSAR$ over
\SI{10}{g}, and absorbed power density, across four phantoms, eight FR1 bands,
three placements, a distance sweep and a device-orientation ensemble:
\num{69216} configurations evaluated in minutes. Normalized local SAR follows
an inverse-square law with a small near-field offset,
$\Sab(d)\propto(d+\delta)^{-2}$ with $\delta\approx\SI{6}{mm}$ and
$R^2>0.999$; device orientation contributes a coefficient of variation of
\SIrange{27}{42}{\percent}, and inter-individual (adult/child) differences add
a further \SIrange{30}{40}{\percent}. Because the whole pipeline is written in a
differentiable array backend, gradients of any metric with respect to the
device pose are exact; we demonstrate sensitivity analysis, gradient-based
orientation optimisation that beats a 512-sample brute-force search, and a
Newton solve for the ICNIRP-compliant stand-off distance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
The dose-model team asked, after the dose-model meetings, for two additions to
the normalized SAR tables: (i) a simple way to adjust a normalized value for the
source-to-body distance, since the gap between a phone and the face strongly
changes the absorbed dose, and (ii) an uncertainty estimate, for example a
standard deviation or a min/max band, for each normalized value such as the
\SI{2.62}{W\per kg\per W} brain figure for the Duke model in the
\texttt{front\_of\_eyes\_center\_vertical} scenario.

A full-wave (FDTD) campaign answers these only at the cost of one solve per
distance and per orientation, which is why distance and orientation have so far
been fixed constants. The surface method below evaluates a single configuration
in milliseconds, so the distance curve and the orientation/phantom distribution
come essentially for free, and the answer is a fitted law plus a full
distribution rather than a single number.

\section{Near-field surface model}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Point-source absorbed power density}
A hand-held antenna is electrically small. For a radiator of largest dimension
$L_a\lesssim\SI{5}{cm}$ the Fraunhofer distance $2L_a^2/\lambda$ is at most a few
centimetres across FR1, so at the \SIrange{8}{200}{mm} holding distances of
interest the wavefront curvature over a single triangle is negligible: the field
is locally a plane wave. We therefore treat the device as a point source that
radiates its measured free-space pattern, and evaluate the absorbed power density
at each body triangle from the AEGIS surface law
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sab}
  \Sab(\mathbf{r})
  = \frac{P_{\mathrm{rad}}\,D(\khat_{\mathrm{ant}}(\mathbf{r}))}
         {4\pi\,d(\mathbf{r})^2}\;
    T_{\mathrm{eff}}\!\bigl(\mu(\mathbf{r})\bigr)\;
    \mathrm{ReLU}\!\bigl(\mu(\mathbf{r})\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $d(\mathbf{r})$ is the source-to-triangle distance, $\khat(\mathbf{r})$ the
line-of-sight direction, $D$ the linear directivity sampled in the antenna frame,
$\mu=\nhat\cdot(-\khat)$ the local incidence cosine, and $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ the
unpolarised Fresnel power transmission of skin. Each triangle thus sees its own
direction, distance and incidence angle, the genuine spherical-wave geometry,
while reusing the AEGIS Fresnel and ReLU-projection physics unchanged. The
directivity patterns are the rigorously re-evaluated \ang{1} grids from the
Sim4Life near-to-far transform of the IFA/PIFA on the handset, one per band.
\Cref{fig:geometry} shows the three placements and \cref{fig:apd} an example
surface map.

\subsection{psSAR10g from the surface field}
ICNIRP below \SI{6}{GHz} restricts the peak spatial-average SAR over a
\SI{10}{g} cube. We obtain it from the surface field with the closed-form
reduction derived in the AEGIS theory notes. The volumetric SAR under a surface
point decays as $\mathrm{SAR}(z)=(2\alpha/\rho_m)\Sab\,e^{-2\alpha z}$ with field
attenuation rate $\alpha=k_0\kappa$; mass-averaging over the cube of side
$L=(m/\rho_m)^{1/3}\approx\SI{21.5}{mm}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pssar}
  \psSAR = \frac{2\alpha}{\rho_m}\,
           \langle\Sab\rangle_{L^2}\,
           h(2\alpha L), \qquad
  h(u)=\frac{1-e^{-u}}{u},
\end{equation}
where $\langle\Sab\rangle_{L^2}$ is the peak of the $\Sab$ field averaged over a
patch of area $L^2$ (computed with the AEGIS spatial-averaging matrix retargeted
to $L^2$). The depth-efficiency factor $h(2\alpha L)$ is the below-\SI{6}{GHz}
correction the thin-skin shortcut omits: at \SI{2.45}{GHz}, $2\alpha L\approx1.9$
and $h\approx0.45$, so the surface-only estimate would otherwise overpredict by
about $2\times$. Whole-body SAR is $\SARwb=P_{\mathrm{abs}}/m_{\mathrm{body}}$
with $P_{\mathrm{abs}}=\sum_i\Sab(\mathbf{r}_i)A_i$.

\subsection{Sweep design}
All quantities are linear in radiated power, so we report everything per
\SI{1}{W} radiated, which is exactly the GOLIAT ``normalized SAR'' convention
(\si{W\per kg} per \si{W}). The sweep covers four phantoms (Duke, Ella, Eartha,
Thelonious), eight FR1 bands (\SIrange{700}{5800}{MHz}), three placements, a
geometric distance grid (\SIrange{5}{400}{mm}) and a device-orientation ensemble
of uniformly random rotations plus the nominal boresight-at-body pose. This is
\num{69216} configurations; the averaging matrices depend only on the mesh and
are built once per phantom, so the orientation ensemble at each distance is a
single batched evaluation.

\section{Distance dependence}
\label{sec:distance}
\Cref{fig:distance} shows the four metrics versus distance for the front-of-eyes
placement at \SI{2450}{MHz}, with the orientation envelope and the fitted law.
The local metrics ($\psSAR$, peak and \SI{4}{cm^2} APD) follow an inverse-square
law with a small near-field offset,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:distlaw}
  S(d) = S(d_{\mathrm{ref}})\,
         \left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{ref}}+\delta}{d+\delta}\right)^{2},
\end{equation}
with $\delta$ of order the source size. Across all placements and bands the fit
quality is excellent (\cref{tab:distfit}): $R^2\ge0.989$ and $\delta$ in the
range \SIrange{3.5}{15.8}{mm} (median \SI{6.9}{mm}). For distances beyond a few
centimetres \cref{eq:distlaw} reduces to the textbook inverse-square rule.
Whole-body SAR is the exception: it depends only weakly on distance, because the
total absorbed power redistributes over the body rather than vanishing, so a flat
or weak power-law description is more appropriate there.

\section{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
For each normalized value we report the mean, standard deviation, min/max and
percentiles over the ensemble. \Cref{tab:unc} summarises the \SI{2450}{MHz}
orientation spread per placement. The device orientation is the dominant
intra-phantom source: the coefficient of variation is \SI{42}{\percent} for
$\psSAR$ front of eyes and \SI{28}{\percent} at the cheek, where the small
stand-off pins the geometry. \Cref{fig:unc} shows the distributions normalised
to the nominal pose; the nominal boresight-at-body pose is not the worst case, so
the median sits above unity for the distant placements.

Inter-individual variability is a second, comparable axis. Across the four
phantoms the nominal front-of-eyes value varies with a CV of \SI{18}{\percent}
for $\psSAR$ and \SI{38}{\percent} for whole-body SAR, the latter because smaller
(child) bodies absorb more per unit mass. The two axes combine in quadrature to a
total of order \SI{45}{\percent}.

\subsection{Application to the cited value}
The distance law and the orientation uncertainty apply directly to the
\SI{2.62}{W\per kg\per W} brain reference, since both are set by the
source-body geometry rather than by which organ is scored. \Cref{tab:applied}
adjusts the reference to other distances via \cref{eq:distlaw} and attaches the
orientation band; \cref{fig:factor} plots it. Halving the distance to
\SI{100}{mm} raises the brain value to about \SI{9.9}{W\per kg\per W}; doubling it
to \SI{400}{mm} lowers it to about \SI{0.68}{W\per kg\per W}. This is the
ready-to-use distance-and-uncertainty answer for the dose model.

\section{Differentiable pipeline}
\label{sec:diff}
The model in \cref{sec:method} is written against a JAX array backend, so the map
from device pose to any metric is differentiable end to end (validated against
finite differences to \SI{<2}{\percent}). Three uses follow.

\paragraph{Sensitivity} A single reverse-mode pass returns the Jacobian of
$[\SARwb,\psSAR,\text{peak APD}]$ against the six pose degrees of freedom
(\cref{fig:poc}, left). Yaw dominates: a \ang{10} yaw changes peak APD by
\SI{30}{\percent}, far more than a \SI{1}{cm} translation, which quantifies why
orientation is the leading uncertainty in \cref{sec:uncertainty}.

\paragraph{Inverse design} Gradient descent on the three orientation angles
(position fixed) finds the pose that minimises head $\psSAR$ in
\textasciitilde\num{60} steps, reaching \SI{4.2e-3}{W\per kg\per W}, below the
minimum of a \num{512}-sample brute-force orientation scan
(\SI{5.1e-3}{}) and $4.5\times$ under the nominal pose (\cref{fig:poc}, right).

\paragraph{Compliance distance} Using the analytic derivative
$\mathrm{d}\,\psSAR/\mathrm{d}\,d$, Newton's method solves for the smallest
stand-off at which a given power stays under the ICNIRP local limit. For a
\SI{250}{mW} handset against the \SI{2}{W\per kg} restriction the front-of-eyes
geometry is compliant beyond \SI{3.2}{mm}, found in seven iterations. The same
machinery turns ``what distance gives dose $X$'' into a one-line solve rather
than a table lookup.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The phantoms are surface meshes without tissue or organ segmentation, so AEGIS
computes surface APD and whole-body SAR directly; the brain figure remains a
volumetric FDTD quantity. The distance law and the orientation/phantom
uncertainty, however, are governed by the source-body geometry and transfer to
any scored organ, which is what makes the application to the \SI{2.62}{} value
defensible. The point-source approximation degrades when the stand-off drops
below the antenna size (a few \si{cm}), so the closest distances should be read
as an upper bound. The psSAR10g reduction assumes a homogeneous skin half-space;
below \SI{1}{GHz}, layered fat/muscle reflections would refine it. Tissue is
modelled as skin throughout, the conservative choice for surface absorption. The
\SI{700}{} and \SI{835}{MHz} efficiencies are unreliable as noted.

\section{Conclusion}
A near-field point-source extension of the AEGIS surface method answers both
dose-model requests directly: normalized local SAR scales as
$(d+\delta)^{-2}$ with $\delta\approx\SI{6}{mm}$, and each value carries an
orientation CV of \SIrange{27}{42}{\percent} with a further
\SIrange{30}{40}{\percent} from inter-individual differences. Because the
pipeline is differentiable, the same model also does sensitivity analysis,
inverse design and compliance-distance solving that a full-wave campaign cannot
afford. All coefficients and statistics are tabulated in the accompanying
workbook \texttt{nearfield\_dose\_results.xlsx}.
