# Nature Career columns from my own work

A different angle from `nature_career_column_ideas.md`. That doc lists 38 generic career
themes (say "I don't know", performing exhaustion, etc.) and checks them against the corpus.
This one starts from the opposite end: the specific, slightly unusual stuff I am actually
living right now (AEGIS, the patent, IOF, the standards play, GOLIAT, the monograph) and asks
which of it is a column.

The whole reason this is worth doing: a project-derived column is automatically original,
because nobody else has my exact stack of decisions. The 38 generic ones risk colliding with
the corpus. These mostly cannot.

---

## The timing axis (Robin's "now or later" point)

The single most useful filter. A Nature Careers column is a first-person story with a landed
lesson. Some of my lessons have already landed, so I can write them now. Others need an
outcome I do not have yet, so they are 2027 columns.

- **NOW** = the realization is complete, I do not need the ending. (The patent saga taught me
  something about *pressure* regardless of whether the patent ever grants.)
- **LATER** = the column only works once I know how it turned out. ("I got my method into an
  international standard" is a great column or a sad one depending on whether IEC says yes.)

A second filter that matters for me specifically and not for a normal postdoc: **founder
reputational risk**. Some of these attach to my name forever while I am doing customer
discovery and sitting near a standards committee. A column that reads as "I cut corners" or
"my university and I are fighting" can cost a deal. Flagged per idea.

---

## Tier 1 — the ones I would actually pitch

### 1. "Nobody was pushing me to patent. It was a deadline wearing a face."
**Source:** IDF / patent saga. **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: low.**

The scene writes itself: a Sunday night in April, me venting that people keep shoving a patent
down my throat, a lawyer about to produce a bureaucratic piece of paper, the whole thing a
hedge I resent. I draft an explosive publish-and-market plan as the rebellion. Then I list who
is actually pushing: Wout is cautious, Anniek recommended the *opposite* (trade secrets), the
TechTransfer office is just processing a queue. There is no cabal. The thing I was angry at was
an EPO novelty deadline standing between me and my defense. I had given the deadline a face and
then resented the face.

**The universal truth:** a lot of what we experience as people pressuring us is actually
structure (deadlines, funding calendars, novelty rules) that we personify because structure is
harder to be angry at. Naming the structure dissolves the resentment and lets you act.

**Why it is fresh:** the corpus has zero columns on patent decisions, and zero on
misattributing structural pressure to colleagues. Grant-stress columns exist; this is not that.

**Titles:** "The cabal that wasn't." / "Nobody was pushing me. It was a deadline." / "I gave a
deadline a face, then resented the face."

---

### 2. "Stop guarding the equations. Nobody is stealing them."
**Source:** AEGIS + the patent-vs-publish reasoning. **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: low-medium.**

I read the classic startup line, *competition is the last reason companies fail, they fail
because they never find customers*, and it rearranged my thinking. For months I treated my
equations as the crown jewels to be locked away. Then I admitted the obvious: the physics
leaves the building anyway, through my thesis, my conference papers, my monograph. The thing
that is actually hard to copy is not the formula. It is the 2,400-test validation suite, the
ingest pipeline, the relationships, the seat near the standards table. I had been protecting
the one asset that was already public and ignoring the moat that was real.

**The universal truth:** young scientist-founders systematically over-value the idea and
under-value the execution scaffolding around it. The instinct to keep secrets is usually
guarding the wrong thing.

**Why it is fresh:** "four pillars of a science spin-off" exists in the corpus but it is
generic. This is a specific, contrarian, slightly painful confession about what a moat actually
is. Pairs naturally with #1 but stands alone.

**Titles:** "I spent a month protecting the wrong thing." / "Your equations are not your moat."

**Risk note:** mildly spicy because it argues against the patent-maximalist line some colleagues
hold. Keep it about *my* misjudgment, not about who was right.

---

### 3. "Why I am publishing the thing I am told to keep secret."
**Source:** the disclosure-as-credibility paradox. **Timing: NOW (decision) or LATER (outcome).** **Risk: HIGH.**

TechTransfer and the funding jury would both prefer I not disclose the theory. I am going to
disclose it anyway, because credibility with customers, with the IEC standards people, and with
peer research groups *requires* the method be in the open literature. A secret method nobody can
check is worthless to a compliance standard. The valorisation logic (protect, then sell) and the
scientific-credibility logic (disclose, then be trusted) point in opposite directions, and I had
to pick a side while standing between them.

**The universal truth:** "valorisation" and "credibility" are sold to PhD students as the same
arrow. They are not. Sometimes the move that builds your scientific reputation is the move your
tech-transfer office actively does not want.

**Why it is fresh:** genuinely uncovered. The entrepreneurship columns assume protect-first.

**Risk note:** this one is *politically live*. Writing publicly about "my university does not
want me to publish" while I am mid-negotiation with that university's TechTransfer is a bad idea
in the moment. This is a **post-defense column (Nov 2026+)** or an anonymized one. Worth writing,
worth holding.

---

### 4. "The most valuable thing I did this year was two paragraphs in a standard."
**Source:** the standards play (IEC/IEEE 63195-2). **Timing: LATER (needs the outcome).** **Risk: low.**

Almost no PhD knows this lever exists. There is a window, right now, measured in months, to get
a computational method named as acceptable in an international exposure standard. Miss it and the
next window is years away. Getting in is worth more than 500 GitHub stars or any single early
customer, because once a method is *in the standard*, practitioners do not get to ignore it. They
have to cite it. It is the quietest, highest-leverage, most time-pressured thing on my entire
list, and it is invisible from inside a normal PhD.

**The universal truth:** the highest-leverage career moves are often the ones with no
recognition ritual. No award, no citation count, no LinkedIn moment. Just a sentence in a
document that quietly reorganizes a field around your work.

**Why it is fresh:** nothing in the corpus on standards bodies as a career instrument. This is a
whole hidden game most academics never learn the rules of.

**Why LATER:** the column is "here is how I got in" or "here is how I missed and what I learned."
Both need the result. The *insight* (this lever exists, the window is closing) is writable now as
advice, but the column wants the story.

**Titles:** "The career move with no trophy." / "I will be prouder of a footnote in a standard
than of any paper."

## Tier 2 — real, slightly softer

### 5. "My partner needs five years here. So I stopped chasing and started building."
**Source:** the geography constraint + IOF / EU-bubble reasoning. **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: low.**

The standard two-body-problem column is about sacrifice and compromise. Mine inverts it.
Karolina needs ~5 years in Belgium. Instead of treating that as a cage shrinking my options, I
treated it as a brief: if I cannot chase the best job *anywhere*, I will build something that
makes location irrelevant, and I will use the low-hours research-fund track (which most people
see as a consolation prize) as the thing I actually want, which is *time to think*. "There is
nothing better than that for doing your analyses." The constraint became the design input.

**The universal truth:** career advice assumes mobility is freedom and constraint is loss.
Sometimes the constraint is what forces the better strategy. A fixed point can be a foundation.

**Why it is fresh:** two-body columns exist (relocation, one-couple-two-cities) but they are
about logistics and compromise. The "constraint as brief, not cage" reframing, plus the specific
EU-research-fund-as-luxury-of-time angle, is not in there.

**Titles:** "The constraint that made my plan better." / "I stopped chasing jobs and started
building one."

---

### 6. "I wrote a book instead of stapling four papers together."
**Source:** the monograph-as-thesis. **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: low.**

Most theses in my field are a stapled compilation of published papers with an intro bolted on.
I wrote a ~6,000-line monograph as a single coherent source of truth, with every table backed by
a regression test and every equation traceable. It was much harder. It was also the thing that
made the whole edifice (papers, patent claims, the company's docs) internally consistent, because
there was one canonical place where the physics had to agree with itself.

**The universal truth:** coherence is a deliverable, not a byproduct. The compilation thesis
optimizes for "done"; the monograph optimizes for "true together", and the second one keeps
paying off long after the defense.

**Why it is fresh:** "write your thesis backwards" exists; "compilation vs monograph, and why I
chose the harder one" does not. Slightly niche (only fields that allow both formats), which is the
main risk against Nature Careers' "not too niche" rule.

**Titles:** "The thesis I made harder on purpose." / "One source of truth."

---

### 7. "I calculate how much radiation your phone puts into your skull."
**Source:** AEGIS dosimetry + GOLIAT (5G exposure / public risk perception). **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: low.**

This is the natural home for GOLIAT. The public is frightened of 5G and radiation. I build the
tool that quantifies exactly how much power a human body absorbs. GOLIAT is partly about citizen
risk perception. So I sit at a strange intersection: the person with the actual numbers, talking
to people for whom the numbers are not the point, the fear is. The column is about what it is
like to be the reassurance-giver on a feared topic, and how having the model changes (and does
not change) the conversation. The numbers turn out to be the easy part.

**The universal truth:** science communication columns teach you to explain well. They rarely
admit that on an emotionally charged topic, being *correct* and being *believed* are different
problems, and that being the expert can make you trusted less, not more.

**Why it is fresh:** sci-comm columns are about technique. This is about the specific burden of
being the calm quantitative voice on a topic the public experiences as a threat. Not in the
corpus.

**Titles:** "I have the numbers. The numbers are the easy part." / "Being the calm voice on a
scary topic."

## Tier 3 — write only if a real story appears

### 8. "I built a company and wrote papers with a swarm of AI agents."
**Source:** the actual way I work (PaperMaker9000, agent orchestration). **Timing: NOW.** **Risk: HIGH for a founder.**

Honest and current, and the corpus's identified gap is exactly "cognitive effects of AI on the
researcher" rather than "here are 3 ChatGPT tips". The fresh version is not "AI is great", it is
"here is the one thing I still refuse to hand to an agent, and why". But: the AI-careers shelf is
the *most crowded* in the whole corpus (25+ columns), so the bar is high, and for *me
specifically* a public "I let agents write my papers" essay is a credibility liability while I am
selling a physics tool whose whole pitch is rigor. Only worth it with a genuinely sharp, narrow
thesis, and probably not under my own name this year.

### 9. "The year everything came due at once."
**Source:** the JSAC race + patent deadline + defense + IOF deadline + book deadline all stacking
into mid-2026. **Timing: LATER.** **Risk: low.** This is a real and visceral story (a sprint
where a paper, a patent, a thesis, and a funding call all share a two-month window) but it is only
a column once I know whether the gamble of doing them simultaneously paid off. Survivorship makes
or breaks it. Hold for 2027.

---

## My recommendation

If I write *one* now: **#1 (the deadline wearing a face)**. Cleanest scene, fully landed lesson,
zero political risk, completely original, and it is the kind of honest-realization piece the
section loves.

If I write *two*: add **#2 (wrong moat)** or **#7 (the radiation numbers / GOLIAT)**. #2 is the
sharper idea; #7 is the safer one and gives GOLIAT a home.

Hold **#3 (publishing the secret)** until after the defense for political safety, and hold **#4
(standards)** and **#9 (everything at once)** until I have the outcomes. Those three are 2027
columns and probably my *best* material once they can be told.

One practical note on timing the writing itself: until the patent priority is filed (~Jul-Aug
2026) I am in a publication blackout on the *method*. None of these columns disclose the method
(they are about process and experience, not physics), so they are technically safe even now. But
#1, #2, #3 all touch the patent process, so the cautious move is to draft now and publish after
the priority is filed, so I am writing about a closed chapter rather than a live negotiation.
