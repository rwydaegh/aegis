## 4. Publish vs. commercialize: IP strategy

### The question

AEGIS is both a scientific contribution (the pseudo-Brewster compensation insight, 9 fidelity levels, the exposure operator Q) and a commercial product (the software, the viewer, the API). The question is how to handle each.

### The Kuster playbook

The closest historical parallel is Niels Kuster at ETH Zurich, who built the dominant EMF dosimetry ecosystem from his PhD work. The pattern:

1. Publish foundational research (credibility, citations)
2. Participate in standards bodies like IEC/IEEE (influence, become the reference)
3. Build measurement hardware via SPEAG (lock-in)
4. Build simulation software via ZMT/Sim4Life (recurring revenue, $15-50K/year licenses)
5. Maintain the IT'IS tissue database as a public good (goodwill, ecosystem dependency)

This took ~20 years to build into a near-monopoly. The first step was always the science. The company came later, built on the credibility.

AEGIS could follow a compressed version of this, accelerated by AI development speed and the Belgian institutional support system.

### The correct sequencing

1. **IDF filed** (done, April 2026). This establishes priority and protects novelty.
2. **Patent assessment** (in progress, An Van den Broecke + Octrooien@UGent.be). TechTransfer and the patent office decide what's patentable. Luc is optimistic. Wout is cautious. The method (not "software") may be patentable as a computational dosimetry procedure.
3. **Publish the math** (after patent filing is resolved). The monograph contains career-defining work. A Nature Communications or IEEE TAP paper on geometric dosimetry would establish AEGIS as the reference framework. This step must wait until the patent question is settled, because publishing destroys novelty.
4. **Commercialize the platform** (in parallel with publishing, via VLAIO/istart). The software, the API, the viewer, the compliance reports. This is the product.

The critical point: publishing and commercializing are not alternatives. They are complements. The publication creates the credibility that makes the commercial product sell. But the order matters: protect first, then publish, then sell.

### Who actually pays (realistic, next 1-3 years)

| Customer type | What they pay for | Price range | Sales cycle | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Test labs** (Eurofins, Verkotan, PCTEST) | Computational dosimetry as a service line, pre-screening before measurement | 10-50K/year | Short (lab director decides) | High |
| **Sim4Life/ZMT integration** | AEGIS as a fast pre-screening module inside Sim4Life | Licensing deal, variable | Medium (Wout has the contact) | Medium-high |
| **Equipment vendors** (Ericsson, Nokia) | Fast compliance evaluation for antenna designs, 10,000 beam configs overnight | 30-100K/year | 12-18 months (starts as research collab) | Medium |
| **Device OEMs** (Apple, Samsung) | Pre-screening before sending devices to compliance labs | 30-100K/year | 12-18 months | Medium |
| **Consulting** | Run AEGIS for clients, deliver compliance reports | 200-500/hour | Immediate | High (but doesn't scale) |

Note: Luc is actively pursuing Ericsson (NDA stage). Wout suggested the Sim4Life path. These are not hypothetical.

### Who does NOT pay (despite sounding logical)

- **Telecom operators** (Proximus, KPN, etc.): They use simple exclusion-zone tools that regulators already accept. No operator is currently required to do body-mesh dosimetry. Not a buyer until regulations tighten or AEGIS is embedded in their existing network planning tools.
- **Regulators**: They set rules, they don't buy simulation tools.
- **Academics**: They want it free. Essential for credibility and citations, not for revenue.

This may change if ICNIRP enforcement tightens or if 6G drives new requirements, but today these are not paying customers.

### The open-core debate

One approach suggested for AEGIS: open-source the core engine (levels 0-6, incoherent dosimetry) and sell the advanced features (levels 7-8, coherent MIMO, viewer, cloud, enterprise reports).

**Arguments for open-core:**
- Open-source levels 0-6 gets adopted by researchers, cited in papers, becomes the reference implementation
- When standards bodies need a computational reference, open AEGIS is the natural choice
- Community contributions and bug reports improve the code
- "Standard reference" status is a moat that's hard to compete with

**Arguments against open-core (and why caution is warranted):**
- Levels 0-6 cover incoherent dosimetry, which is what ~95% of practical compliance cases need today. Open-sourcing 0-6 means giving away the product that test labs and equipment vendors would actually pay for. You'd be open-sourcing the revenue and selling the research frontier.
- With AI-assisted development in 2026, a competitor with the open-source code and the published monograph could build a competing commercial layer faster than in previous decades.
- The monograph is the recipe. Open-sourcing the code while the monograph is public gives away both the recipe and the cooked meal.
- The Kuster model worked through ecosystem lock-in (standards + hardware + software + data), not through open-source goodwill. The IT'IS tissue database is free, but Sim4Life is $15-50K/year.

**Current recommendation:** Do not decide on open-core now. Keep the code proprietary (it already is). Publish the math (after patent resolution). Let the commercial traction from test labs and the Ericsson/Sim4Life conversations inform whether opening the core creates more value than it gives away. This decision can be made at year 2-3 with real market data, not as a theoretical exercise today.

### The market timing question

mmWave (5G FR2) deployment has slowed in the US (T-Mobile returned spectrum, Verizon pivoted to C-band). This is real. Most 5G worldwide is sub-6 GHz today, where SAR (not Sab) is the metric and existing tools work fine.

However, the EU context is different:
- European ICNIRP 2020 adoption is ongoing, and the EU Digital Strategy explicitly addresses 5G EMF
- Sub-6 GHz compliance is also relevant (AEGIS works 100 MHz to 100 GHz, not just mmWave)
- The Sim4Life integration path doesn't depend on mmWave deployment
- 6G research is accelerating, and dosimetry tooling will be needed before 6G launches

The market is small today but growing. The Belgian funding system (VLAIO mandate salary + istart + IOF) means you can survive on a small market while it grows. You don't need VC-scale revenue in year 1. You need enough traction to prove the model works.

### Standards body participation

Wout Joseph is already positioned in the ICNIRP/IEC/IEEE ecosystem. Through him, AEGIS can be proposed as a computational reference for IEC/IEEE 63195 (which now explicitly covers computational procedures alongside measurement). Getting AEGIS cited in a standard is worth more than any marketing spend. This is the Kuster playbook step 2, and you have a direct path to it through your promotor.

## 5. The VLAIO industrial mentor: how it works

### Why this exists

The VLAIO Innovation Mandate is structured as a consortium: a Flemish knowledge institution (UGent), a postdoctoral researcher (you), and an industrial partner. On the spin-off track, there is no existing company partner. Instead, VLAIO requires an industrial mentor: one person with industry experience who coaches you on the commercial side.

### What the mentor formally does

- Coaches the mandate holder on valorization (turning research into a product)
- Participates in the "strategic orientation of the research" toward market needs
- Is attached to some organization (a company, VC fund, or consultancy)
- Signs off on progress reports

### What VLAIO requires

A signed cooperation agreement between UGent and the mentor's organization must be submitted to VLAIO within 4 weeks of the application deadline. This is a formal requirement, not optional.

### Who can be the mentor on a spin-off track

For spin-off mandates specifically, the industrial mentor can be:
- An employee of a relevant company
- Someone from a VC fund (e.g., an istart coach, a PMV contact)
- Someone from a consultancy firm

The mentor does not need to be from a company that will buy the product. They need to be someone with industry experience who can guide on the business side.

| Option | Why | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|
