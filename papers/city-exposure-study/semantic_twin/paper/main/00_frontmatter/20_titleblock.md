% NEXT: Street-level radiofrequency exposure at 15 GHz depends on the route geometry,
% NEXT: surface materials, source support, and body orientation. This study uses
% NEXT: registered street panoramas and a 250 m-radius photogrammetric support mesh to assign
% NEXT: material evidence around five selected pedestrian routes. A common source
% NEXT: measure sets the expected source count from areal density and weights the
% NEXT: observed route-aligned roofline by physical arc length. All exposure values are
% NEXT: normalized per unit areal density times effective isotropic radiated power.
% NEXT: Transport has exact direct, exact first-order specular, and stochastic
% NEXT: first-diffuse terms. Each stochastic first-diffuse path ends at that
% NEXT: interaction.
% NEXT: The directional fields are coupled to a 56,024-element Duke body surface. The
% NEXT: five routes contain 73 standpoints. Each standpoint uses 16 independent
% NEXT: replicas, 200,000 primary rays per replica, and 4,096 fixed first-diffuse angular cells. In
% NEXT: a controlled open-square case, the maximum bounced-term difference between the
% NEXT: adjoint estimator and deterministic quadrature is 0.0616 dB, and the maximum
% NEXT: total-transport difference between the adjoint and forward tracers is 0.0344
% NEXT: dB. The route medians of normalized whole-body specific
% NEXT: absorption rate differ by a factor of 13.34. Direct transport is the largest
% NEXT: term at all 67 nonshadowed standpoints. First-diffuse transport gives the only
% NEXT: nonzero modeled contribution at six shadowed standpoints. The maximum 12-to-16-replica change
% NEXT: in total transfer is 0.0436 dB across the five sites, although lower-tail
% NEXT: estimates in Mexico City and Tokyo are less stable. The result is a conditional
% NEXT: comparison of five selected routes, not a city ranking or a deployed-network
% NEXT: estimate.
% NEXT: \end{abstract}
