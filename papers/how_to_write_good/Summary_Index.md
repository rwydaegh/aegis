## SECTION 2: When and how to write each article section

### CHAPTER 4: Results as a "story": the key driver of an article

**Key Concepts:**
- Results govern content and structure of the whole article
- Identify a clearly connected story leading to take-home messages
- Focus on tables and figures first
- Write bullet points highlighting main message(s) of each figure/table
- Sort figures and tables into best order to connect story pieces
- Reach agreement with co-authors on:
  - Which data to include
  - Important points forming the story
  - Take-home message(s)

**Questions to Focus Drafting:**
- What do my results say?
- What do these results mean in their context?
- Who needs to know about these results?
- Why do they need to know?

---

### CHAPTER 5: Results: turning data into knowledge

**Data Presentation Principles:**
- Illustrate the story
- Present evidence to support or reject hypothesis
- Record important data and metadata
- Present all necessary data with most important points most prominent
- Decide which datasets or details to leave out
- Number and present figures/tables sequentially
- Refer to figures/tables in order
- Tables and figures should "stand alone"

**Choosing Between Figures, Tables, or Text:**
- Tables: recording data, explaining calculations, showing actual values and precision, multiple comparisons
- Figures: overall trend, comprehension through shape, simple comparisons between few elements
- Consider: number vs shape, individual values vs overall pattern, accuracy/precision importance

**Designing Figures:**
- Design around the point you want to get across most strongly
- Determine design elements before using graphics package
- Consider: variable prominence, emphasizing differences vs similarities, meaningful scales/intervals
- Figure types: pie charts (proportions), column/bar charts (independent categories), line charts (sequences/dependent relationships), radar charts (non-comparable categories)
- Keep consistent styles throughout article
- Keep same symbols and order for treatments/variables
- Keep figures free from clutter
- Shrink figures to journal standard size and check clarity
- Appealing figures: 3:2 proportions, boxed when little ink, unboxed when numerous elements

**Common Figure Weaknesses:**
- Wrong figure type chosen
- Weak descriptive titles instead of story-telling titles
- Data repeated from text or tables
- Symbols/lines don't emphasize main results
- Unnecessary clutter
- Axes not labelled descriptively
- Numbers included when exact values not important
- Data categories not sorted to show priorities

**Designing Tables:**
- Record data and metadata
- Good design: layout, choice of data, ordering, clear headings and titles
- Keep free of clutter
- Define abbreviations in title or footnotes
- Don't box tables
- Use horizontal lines as separators
- Use space to separate columns

**Common Table Weaknesses:**
- Weak descriptive titles instead of story-telling titles
- Unnecessary or redundant data
- Non-significant or over-precise numbers
- Omission of necessary data for calculations
- Table not arranged to highlight significant results
- Data not sorted to show important relationships

**Figure Legends Structure:**
1. Title summarizing what figure is about
2. Details of results or models shown
3. Additional explanation of components/methods/essential details
4. Description of units or statistical notation
5. Explanation of symbols or notation

**Table Titles:**
- Include elements 1-4 from figure legends
- Usually brief Parts 2 and 3
- Usually no Part 5

### CHAPTER 6: Writing about results

**Functions of Results Sentences:**
- Highlight important findings
- Locate figure(s) or table(s) where results can be found
- Comment on (but don't discuss) the results

**Verb Tense in Results Sections:**
- Past tense: focuses on completed study (what was done and found)
- Present tense: describes "always true" situation; focuses on document
- Modal verbs: may be used in comments, especially in that clauses

**Results vs Discussion:**
- Check journal Instructions to Contributors for format preference
- Separate Results and Discussion more common
- Confine comments in Results to what numbers show
- Avoid comparing with other research or suggesting explanations in Results section

---

### CHAPTER 7: The Methods section

**Purpose:**
- Establish credibility for results
- Provide enough information for readers to evaluate results
- Enable readers to decide if results mean what author claims
- Referees check: Do methods conform to acceptable scientific standards?

**Organizing Methods Sections:**
- Strategy 1: Use identical or similar subheadings in Methods and Results
- Strategy 2: Use introductory phrases/sentences relating to aims
- Use first sentence of new paragraph to introduce topic and relate to previous content

**Published Methods:**
- Can be cited without detail unless changes made
- If original not readily available, give details plus citation
- Include language of publication in reference list if appropriate
- Novel methods should be described in full

**Active vs Passive Voice:**
- Active: subject does the action
- Passive: subject receives the action, agent often omitted
- Factors influencing choice:
  - Need to know who/what carried out action?
  - Sound repetitive/immodest with personal pronoun?
  - Help information flow?
- Avoid top-heavy sentences (very long subjects, short verbs at end)
- Get subject and verb within first nine words
- Put lists at end of sentence
- Abbreviate passive sentences to avoid repetition

**Common Problems:**
- Top-heavy passive sentences
- Very long subjects before verb

### CHAPTER 8: The Introduction

**Five Stages:**
1. Statements about the field of research (setting/context, claim centrality/importance)
2. More specific statements about aspects already studied (foundation of known information)
3. Statements indicating need for more investigation (gap/research niche)
4. Statements giving purpose/objectives or outlining main activity/findings
5. Optional statements giving positive value/justification

**Stage 1: Locating Your Project**
- Begin with broad statements accepted as fact
- Use present tense for "always true" information
- Use present perfect for extended past to present
- Move from broad to specific (country → province → city)
- Link sentences through positioning of old and new information
- Old information: reader already knows, placed at beginning
- New information: comes towards end

**Using References:**
- Citation methods:
  - Information prominent: focus on information
  - Author prominent: name of author given prominence
  - Weak author prominent: ideas given prominence, names not in main part
- Secondary citation: (Smith 1962, cited in Jones 2002)
- Only cite papers actually read

**Avoiding Plagiarism:**
- Be aware of situations where inadvertent plagiarism likely
- Develop effective note-taking practices
- Make clear whether idea is yours or from another person
- Cite sources appropriately
- Direct quotations extremely rare in science writing
- Paraphrase rather than copy verbatim
- Use sentence templates (remove content, reuse shell)

**Stage 3: Indicating the Gap**
- Can be written in multitude of ways
- Often broad gap early, specific gap near end
- Signal words: however, remains a major challenge, rarely, not well understood, presently unclear

**Stage 4: Statement of Purpose**
- Sets up readers' expectations
- Form: aim/purpose or principal activity/finding
- Considerable flexibility in wording
- Keep list of possible wordings

**Drafting Process:**
1. Begin with Stage 4
2. Draft Stage 3 next
3. Think about how to begin Stage 1
4. Arrange literature information into Stage 2
5. Combine stages into coherent Introduction

**Editing for Logical Flow - Strategy 1: Always Introduce Ideas**
- Use informative titles, subheadings, introduction sections
- Set up expectations, then meet them
- Use first sentence as topic sentence
- Topic sentences link upcoming paragraph to previous one

**Strategy 2: Move from General to Specific**
- Readers expect general information first
- Then details, examples, specific information

**Strategy 3: Put Old Information Before New**
- Old information: reader already knows
- New information: comes later
- Place old information at beginning of sentences

**Strategy 4: Make Link Within First 7-9 Words**
- Connect sentences early
- Enhances readability
- Use passive voice if needed to improve flow

**Strategy 5: Include Verb and Subject in First 7-9 Words**
- Avoid top-heavy sentences
- Long subjects before verb make sentences difficult to follow

### CHAPTER 9: The Discussion section

**Structural Issues:**
- Check if journal allows combined Results/Discussion section
- Check if journal permits Conclusion where Discussion is long
- Check if journal publishes Discussion sections with subheadings
- Relate Discussion closely to paper's title
- Relate Discussion closely to Introduction
- Ensure Discussion connects with Introduction issues
- Don't repeat information unnecessarily

**Information Elements:**
1. Reference to main purpose/hypothesis or summary of main activity
2. Restatement of most important findings (in order of significance):
   - Whether they support original hypothesis
   - How they contribute to main activity/research questions/objectives
   - Whether they agree with other researchers' findings
3. Explanations for findings (supported by references) and/or speculations (supported by citations)
4. Limitations restricting generalization beyond study conditions
5. Implications (generalizations: what results mean in broader context)
6. Recommendations for future research and/or practical applications

**Negotiating Strength of Claims:**
- Match strength of sentences with strength of data and arguments
- Two opportunities in sentences using "that":
  - Choice of vocabulary and tense in main verb
  - Choice of verb tense in that clause
- Strong verbs: demonstrate, indicate (present tense)
- Weak verbs: appears, suggests (with modals)
- Alternative construction: noun phrase as object (no that clause)

**Verb Strength:**
- Strong: demonstrate, indicate (present tense, present/future in that clause)
- Moderate: appears (present tense in that clause)
- Weak: suggests (with modal verbs in that clause)

### CHAPTER 10: The title

**Strategy 1: Provide Relevant Information, Be Concise**
- Attract busy readers in target audience
- More revealing title = easier for readers to judge relevance
- Concise and informative

**Strategy 2: Use Keywords Prominently**
- Decide which words capture attention
- Place keywords near front of title
- Helps with literature-scanning services
- Place most important word(s) at beginning
- Use colon (:) or dash (–) to separate keyword section from explanatory section

**Strategy 3: Choose Grammatical Form**
- Noun phrase: traditional, brief, informative
- Statement: gives explicit information about results, suitable for specific question with non-complex answer
- Question: effective when no simple answer
- Check journal conventions
- Develop list of possible titles, choose most effective at end

**Strategy 4: Avoid Ambiguity in Noun Phrases**
- Restrict to maximum of three words
- Only if no risk of misunderstanding
- If longer, rewrite with prepositions (of, by, for)
- Nouns used as adjectives always singular

### CHAPTER 11: The Abstract

**Why Abstracts Are Important:**
- May be only part busy readers read
- May be only information available in developing countries
- Used by abstracting services for searchable databases

**Selecting Keywords:**
- Consult similar papers in field
- Select from list used by relevant indexing services
- Think about audience and their interests
- Predict keywords audience might use

**Typical Information Elements:**
- Background information (B)
- Principal activity/purpose and scope (P)
- Methods used (M)
- Most important results (R)
- Conclusion or recommendation (C)

**Compressed Components:**
- Principal activity/purpose and method (P + M)
- Results (R)
- Conclusion and recommendations (C)

**Journal-Specific Guidelines:**
- Check Instructions to Contributors for word limits
- Check format requirements
- Some journals require numbered statements
- Some require "Synthesis" section

## SECTION 4: Developing your publication skills further

### CHAPTER 17: Developing discipline-specific English skills

**Key Principles:**
- Good science is most important
- Science needs to be clearly understandable
- Write short sentences first (two clauses only), join later if needed
- Develop repertoire of ways of expressing meanings useful in discipline

**Types of English Errors:**
1. Incorrect usage of singular/plural forms
2. Over-complex/inaccurate grammatical structures
3. Non-agreement of verbs and subjects
4. Incorrect choice of preposition
5. Non-standard usage of articles (a/an, the)
6. Non-standard selection of modal verbs
7. Incorrect choice of part of speech
8. Non-conventional selection of tense

**Sentence Templates:**
- Separate structure/framework from content chunks (noun phrases)
- Remove noun phrases to create reusable templates
- List and characterize noun phrases crossed out
- Identify templates when reading papers
- Record in special file or notebook
- Organize by section of paper

**Noun Phrases:**
- Group of words without finite verb, built around single headword
- Long noun phrases made of smaller phrases joined with prepositions
- Noun-noun phrases: second part moved in front, written in singular form, preposition omitted
- Identify headword first (most important part)
- Skip unfamiliar noun phrases on first reading, focus on sentence structures
- Identify recurring noun phrases, look up meanings as needed

**Concordancing:**
- Tool for developing discipline-specific English
- Searches corpus for examples of search item
- Displays results with search term highlighted
- Can sort by what's on left or right of search term
- Construct corpus of 10+ published research articles from your discipline
- Articles should be: from reputable journals, well-written, cover range of subtopics, appropriate types
- Prepare documents: text-only format (ASCII), delete tables/figures/biodata/references
- PDF conversion: copy/paste procedure, avoid headers/footers/page numbers

**Using Articles (a/an, the):**
- Depends on generic vs specific sense
- Generic noun phrases (any/all members):
  - Countable plural: no article
  - Countable singular: a/an
  - Uncountable: no article
  - Singular countable with "the" (living things, machinery)
- Specific noun phrases (particular members):
  - Referring to shared/assumed knowledge
  - Pointing back to old information
  - Pointing forward to specifying information
- Structure np1 + of + np2: first noun phrase specific about 85% of time

**Using Which and That:**
- Defining relative clauses: essential to meaning, no commas, UK allows which/that, US only that
- Non-defining relative clauses: non-essential, separated by commas, only which
- Test: try inserting "by the way" after which
- Same distinction when which + verb omitted

## General Writing Strategies

**Information Flow:**
- Old information before new information
- General information before specific information
- Link sentences within first 7-9 words
- Include verb and subject in first 7-9 words
- Use topic sentences to orient readers

**Citation:**
- Information prominent (default in many areas)
- Author prominent (use sparingly, approaching gap)
- Weak author prominent (topic sentences, present perfect)
- Choose method to fit argument advancement

**Avoiding Common Problems:**
- Top-heavy sentences
- Unclear information flow
- Weak titles
- Redundant data presentation
- Inappropriate verb tense
- Mismatched claim strength with data strength

**Review Process:**
- Check against Instructions to Contributors
- Analyze example articles from your field
- Refine guidelines based on your discipline
- Compare with published papers
- Discuss with colleagues or teachers

### CHAPTER II: ELEMENTARY RULES OF USAGE

**Rule 1: Form the possessive singular of nouns with 's**
- Follow rule whatever the final consonant
- Exceptions: ancient proper names in -es and -is, possessive Jesus', forms like for conscience' sake
- Pronominal possessives (hers, its, theirs, yours, oneself) have no apostrophe

**Rule 2: In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last**
- Use comma after each term except last
- In business firm names, last comma omitted
- Abbreviation etc. always preceded by comma

**Rule 3: Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas**
- Difficult to apply; hard to decide if single word or phrase is parenthetic
- If interruption slight, may omit commas
- Never omit one comma and leave the other
- Non-restrictive relative clauses set off by commas
- Similar clauses introduced by where and when similarly punctuated
- Restrictive relative clauses not set off by commas
- Abbreviations etc. and jr. always preceded by comma, and followed by one except at end of sentence
- If parenthetic expression preceded by conjunction, place first comma before conjunction

**Rule 4: Place a comma before and or but introducing an independent clause**
- Two-part sentences with second member introduced by as (because), for, or, nor, and while (and at the same time) require comma before conjunction
- If dependent clause or introductory phrase precedes second independent clause, no comma needed after conjunction

**Rule 5: Do not join independent clauses by a comma**
- If clauses grammatically complete and not joined by conjunction, use semicolon
- Equally correct to write as two sentences with periods
- If conjunction inserted, use comma
- If second clause preceded by adverb (accordingly, besides, so, then, therefore, thus), semicolon still required
- Avoid using so in this manner
- If clauses very short and alike in form, comma usually permissible

**Rule 6: Do not break sentences in two**
- Do not use periods for commas
- Permissible to make emphatic word or expression serve purpose of sentence and punctuate accordingly
- Writer must be certain emphasis is warranted

**Rule 7: A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject**
- Participial phrases preceded by conjunction or preposition, nouns in apposition, adjectives, and adjective phrases come under same rule if they begin sentence

**Rule 8: Divide words at line-ends, in accordance with their formation and pronunciation**
- Divide word unless cutting off only single letter or only two letters of long word
- Divide according to formation
- Divide on the vowel
- Divide between double letters, unless they come at end of simple form of word

### CHAPTER III: ELEMENTARY PRINCIPLES OF COMPOSITION

**Rule 9: Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic**
- If subject slight extent or treated briefly, may be single paragraph
- After paragraph written, examine to see if subdivision will improve it
- Ordinarily subject requires subdivision into topics, each made subject of paragraph
- Beginning of each paragraph signals new step in development
- Extent of subdivision varies with length of composition
- Single sentences should not be written or printed as paragraphs (exception: sentences of transition)
- In dialogue, each speech is paragraph by itself

**Rule 10: As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning**
- Most useful paragraph: topic sentence at or near beginning; succeeding sentences explain/establish/develop statement; final sentence emphasizes thought or states important consequence
- Avoid ending with digression or unimportant detail
- Relation to what precedes may need expression (word/phrase in topic sentence, or preceding transitional sentences)
- Body of paragraph may relate to topic sentence by: restating in other forms, defining terms, denying converse, giving illustrations, establishing by proofs, developing by showing implications
- In narration and description, paragraph sometimes begins with concise comprehensive statement
- More commonly opening sentence indicates by subject what paragraph principally concerned
- Brief paragraphs of animated narrative often without topic sentence

**Rule 11: Use the active voice**
- Active voice usually more direct and vigorous than passive
- Rule does not mean entirely discard passive voice (frequently convenient, sometimes necessary)
- Need of making particular word subject often determines which voice to use
- Habitual use of active voice makes for forcible writing
- Avoid making one passive depend directly upon another
- Common fault: use as subject of passive construction a noun expressing entire action, leaving verb no function beyond completing sentence

**Rule 12: Put statements in positive form**
- Make definite assertions
- Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, non-committal language
- Use word not as means of denial or in antithesis, never as means of evasion
- Better to express negative in positive form
- Antithesis of negative and positive is strong
- Negative words other than not usually strong

**Rule 13: Omit needless words**
- Vigorous writing is concise
- Sentence should contain no unnecessary words, paragraph no unnecessary sentences
- Every word should tell
- Expression "the fact that" should be revised out of every sentence
- Who is, which was, and the like often superfluous
- Positive statement more concise than negative
- Active voice more concise than passive
- Common violation: presenting single complex idea step by step in series of sentences that might be combined

**Rule 14: Avoid a succession of loose sentences**
- Refers especially to loose sentences of particular type: two co-ordinate clauses, second introduced by conjunction or relative
- Single sentences of this type may be unexceptionable, but series becomes monotonous
- Recast enough to remove monotony: simple sentences, sentences joined by semicolon, periodic sentences, sentences of three clauses

**Rule 15: Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form**
- Principle of parallel construction: expressions of similar content and function should be outwardly similar
- Unskilful writer violates from mistaken belief should constantly vary form
- Article or preposition applying to all members of series must either be used only before first term or repeated before each term
- Correlative expressions should be followed by same grammatical construction
- If writer needs to express very large number of similar ideas, classify in groups and apply principle within each group

**Rule 16: Keep related words together**
- Position of words in sentence is principal means of showing relationship
- Bring together words and groups of words related in thought
- Keep apart those not so related
- Subject and principal verb should not be separated by phrase or clause that can be transferred to beginning
- Relative pronoun should come immediately after its antecedent
- If antecedent consists of group of words, relative comes at end of group unless causes ambiguity
- Noun in apposition may come between antecedent and relative
- Modifiers should come next to word they modify
- If several expressions modify same word, arrange so no wrong relation suggested

**Rule 17: In summaries, keep to one tense**
- In summarizing action of drama, always use present tense
- In summarizing poem, story, or novel, preferably use present, though may use past
- If summary in present tense, antecedent action expressed by perfect; if in past, by past perfect
- Past tense in indirect discourse or indirect question remains unchanged
- Whichever tense chosen, use throughout
- Avoid intercalating expressions like "he said," "he stated"
- In criticism or interpretation of literature, avoid dropping into summary
- If scope includes number of works, aim from beginning at establishing general conclusions

**Rule 18: Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end**
- Proper place for word or group of words writer desires to make most prominent is usually end of sentence
- Word or group entitled to this position usually logical predicate (new element)
- Effectiveness of periodic sentence arises from prominence given to main statement
- Other prominent position is beginning
- Any element other than subject becomes emphatic when placed first
- Subject coming first may be emphatic, but not by position alone
- To receive special emphasis, subject must take position of predicate
- Principle applies to words of sentence, sentences of paragraph, paragraphs of composition

### CHAPTER IV: A FEW MATTERS OF FORM

**Headings:**
- Leave blank line after title or heading
- On succeeding pages, begin on first line

**Numerals:**
- Do not spell out dates or serial numbers
- Write in figures or Roman notation as appropriate

**Parentheses:**
- Sentence containing expression in parenthesis punctuated outside marks exactly as if expression absent
- Expression within punctuated as if stood by itself, except final stop omitted unless question mark or exclamation point
- When wholly detached expression or sentence parenthesized, final stop comes before last mark

**Quotations:**
- Formal quotations introduced by colon and enclosed in quotation marks
- Quotations grammatically in apposition or direct objects of verbs preceded by comma and enclosed in quotation marks
- Quotations of entire line or more of verse begun on fresh line and centred, not enclosed in quotation marks
- Quotations introduced by that regarded as indirect discourse, not enclosed in quotation marks
- Proverbial expressions and familiar phrases of literary origin require no quotation marks
- Same true of colloquialisms and slang

**References:**
- In scholarly work, abbreviate titles occurring frequently, giving full forms in alphabetical list at end
- Give references in parenthesis or footnotes, not in body of sentence
- Omit words act, scene, line, book, volume, page except when referring by only one

**Titles:**
- For titles of literary works, scholarly usage prefers italics with capitalized initials
- Use italics (indicated in manuscript by underscoring) except in writing for periodical following different practice
- Omit initial A or The from titles when placing possessive before them

### CHAPTER V: WORDS AND EXPRESSIONS COMMONLY MISUSED

**Common Misuses:**
- All right: always written as two words
- As good or better than: correct by rearranging sentence
- As to whether: whether is sufficient
- Bid: takes infinitive without to; past tense is bade
- Case: usually unnecessary
- Certainly: used indiscriminately, bad in speech, worse in writing
- Character: often simply redundant
- Claim: with object-noun means lay claim to; not substitute for declare, maintain, or charge
- Compare: compare to (resemblances, different order); compare with (differences, same order)
- Clever: best restricted to ingenuity in small matters
- Consider: not followed by as when means "believe to be"
- Dependable: needless substitute for reliable, trustworthy
- Due to: incorrectly used for through, because of, or owing to in adverbial phrases
- Effect: as noun means result; as verb means to bring about, accomplish (not confused with affect)
- Etc.: not used of persons; not used if reader left in doubt; incorrect at end of list introduced by such as, for example
- Fact: use only of matters capable of direct verification, not matters of judgment
- Factor: hackneyed word; expressions usually replaced by something more direct
- Feature: hackneyed word; as verb in advertising sense to be avoided
- Fix: colloquial for arrange, prepare, mend; in writing restrict to literary senses
- He is a man who: common type of redundant expression
- However: in meaning nevertheless, not to come first in sentence or clause
- Kind of: not substitute for rather or something like (except in familiar style)
- Less: should not be misused for fewer (less refers to quantity, fewer to number)
- Line, along these lines: overworked, better discarded entirely
- Literal, literally: often incorrectly used in support of exaggeration
- Lose out: meant to be more emphatic than lose, but actually less so
- Most: not to be used for almost
- Nature: often simply redundant, used like character
- Near by: adverbial phrase, not fully accepted; not used as adjective (use neighboring)
- Oftentimes, ofttimes: archaic forms, use often
- One hundred and one: retain and in this and similar expressions
- One of the most: avoid beginning essays or paragraphs with this formula
- People: the people is political term, not confused with the public; not used with words of number (use persons)
- Phase: means stage of transition or development; not used for aspect or topic
- Possess: not mere substitute for have or own
- Respective, respectively: may usually be omitted with advantage
- So: avoid use as intensifier in writing
- Sort of: see under Kind of
- State: not mere substitute for say, remark; restrict to sense of express fully or clearly
- Student body: needless and awkward expression
- System: frequently used without need
- Thanking you in advance: sounds as if writer meant it not worth while to write again
- They: common inaccuracy using plural pronoun when antecedent is distributive expression (each, every one, etc.)
- Very: use sparingly; where emphasis necessary, use words strong in themselves
- Viewpoint: write point of view, but do not misuse for view or opinion
- While: avoid indiscriminate use for and, but, and although; use only with strict literalness (during the time that)
- Whom: often incorrectly used for who before he said or similar expressions
- Worth while: overworked as term of vague approval; strictly applicable only to actions
- Would: conditional statement in first person requires should, not would; equivalent of shall in indirect quotation after verb in past tense is should, not would; to express habitual action, past tense usually sufficient

### CHAPTER VI: WORDS OFTEN MISSPELLED

**Commonly Misspelled Words:**
- accidentally, advice, affect, beginning, believe, benefit, challenge, criticize, deceive, definite, describe, despise, develop, disappoint, duel, ecstasy, effect, existence, fiery, formerly, humorous, hypocrisy, immediately, incidentally, latter, led, lose, marriage, mischief, murmur, necessary, occurred, parallel, Philip, playwright, preceding, prejudice, principal, privilege, pursue, repetition, rhyme, rhythm, ridiculous, sacrilegious, seize, separate, shepherd, siege, similar, simile, too, tragedy, tries, undoubtedly, until

**Hyphenation:**
- Write to-day, to-night, to-morrow (but not together) with hyphen
- Write any one, every one, some one, some time (except sense of formerly) as two words
