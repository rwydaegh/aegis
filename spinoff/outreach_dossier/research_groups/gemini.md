Global Academic and Institutional Hierarchy in Computational Dosimetry: Advancements in FDTD, Hybrid Ray-Tracing, and Machine-Learning-Driven Exposure AssessmentThe rapid evolution of wireless telecommunication networks—transitioning from the localized beamforming configurations of fifth-generation (5G) New Radio (NR) networks to the highly distributed, cell-free massive Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (MIMO) topologies of emerging sixth-generation (6G) systems—has fundamentally altered the physical paradigms of human electromagnetic field (EMF) exposure assessment. Historically, regulatory compliance of base stations (BS) and user equipment (UE) was governed by conservative, static "worst-case" assumptions. Modern dosimetry, however, requires high-fidelity, dynamic in silico evaluations that capture the temporal and spatial interactions between complex propagation environments and heterogeneous biological systems.This technical transition is closely aligned with updates in international exposure safety standards. The guidelines established by the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP 2020) and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers International Committee on Electromagnetic Safety (IEEE ICES C95.1-2019) have updated the dosimetric quantities required for human safety evaluations across different frequency bands.For frequencies below $6\text{ GHz}$, Specific Absorption Rate (SAR), representing the mass-normalized rate of RF energy absorption in tissue, remains the primary physical restriction :$$SAR = \frac{\sigma |\mathbf{E}|^2}{2\rho} = C \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}$$where $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the tissue ($\text{S/m}$), $\mathbf{E}$ is the peak internal electric field vector ($\text{V/m}$), $\rho$ is the tissue mass density ($\text{kg/m}^3$), $C$ is the specific heat capacity ($\text{J/kg}\cdot\text{K}$), and $\partial T / \partial t$ is the initial rate of temperature rise.Above the transition frequency of $6\text{ GHz}$ (extending up to $300\text{ GHz}$), the limited penetration depth of RF fields shifts the primary biophysical hazard from core heating to superficial tissue temperature elevation. Consequently, absorbed power density ($S_{ab}$ or APD), representing the power penetrating the body per unit area, is established as the primary physical restriction :$$S_{ab} = \iint_{A} \operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}^*\} \cdot d\mathbf{a}$$where the Poynting vector is integrated over a defined averaging area $A$ (typically $4\text{ cm}^2$ for local exposure) at the body surface.To model these physical interactions, academic and institutional research groups have pioneered advanced computational frameworks. These frameworks integrate Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) solvers, deterministic ray-tracing, machine-learning (ML) surrogates, and real-time computational digital twins to assess exposure from base stations and mobile terminals.Academic and Institutional Hierarchy in Computational DosimetryThe global landscape of computational dosimetry is led by a select group of highly specialized academic institutions and research foundations. They maintain substantial research footprints, co-develop industry-standard simulation software, and hold leadership positions within international safety standard committees.IT'IS Foundation / ETH Zurich (Switzerland)The IT'IS Foundation (Information Technologies in Society), operating as a close partner of ETH Zurich, represents the largest and most influential computational dosimetry entity globally. Co-founded and historically led by Niels Kuster, the institution has successfully bridged academic research with commercial software toolchains through its spin-off companies Schmid & Partner Engineering AG (SPEAG) and ZMT Zurich MedTech AG.IT'IS is the primary developer of the Virtual Population (ViP), a suite of highly detailed, functionalized anatomical human and animal models (including the widely utilized "Duke" and "Yoon-sun" phantoms). The ViP models feature advanced posing capabilities—such as realistic joint deformations and spinal articulation—allowing for precise in silico exposure assessments. The technical toolchain is built around Sim4Life, an advanced multi-physics and multi-physiology simulation platform co-developed with ZMT. Sim4Life integrates robust electromagnetic solvers—specifically highly parallelized FDTD implementations with customized boundary conditions—with thermodynamic tissue solvers based on the Pennes Bioheat Transfer Equation (BHTE).The group's recent focus centers on validating traceable assessment protocols for absorbed power density ($S_{ab}$) above $10\text{ GHz}$, establishing detailed multi-layered skin dielectric models up to $110\text{ GHz}$ to model the extreme near-field interactions of body-worn devices and millimeter-wave transmitters. Furthermore, their material parameter databases (such as Tissue Properties Database V5.0) provide the international scientific community with updated estimates of electromagnetic, thermal, and fluidic properties of biological tissues.Ghent University - imec (WAVES / IDLab) (Belgium)The WAVES and IDLab research groups at Ghent University and imec, led by investigators including Wout Joseph, Günter Vermeeren, Luc Martens, and David Plets, have pioneered computational dosimetry for complex, dynamic telecommunication scenarios. The group focuses on the transition from static, conservative "worst-case" assumptions to realistic, time-averaged exposure evaluations in 5G-NR and 6G networks.WAVES has developed a hybrid propagation-to-exposure workflow designed for mmWave massive MIMO networks. The deterministic propagation environment is modeled via multi-bounce Ray Tracing (RT) utilizing 3D urban models sourced from high-resolution geographic information systems. The output of this ray-tracer is hybridized using a virtual "Huygens' box" interface, which records the complex incident fields surrounding a user’s path. This boundary condition then excites a high-resolution FDTD solver containing realistic anatomical phantoms to calculate localized SAR and surface APD ($S_{ab}$).The group's hybrid workflows have been utilized to evaluate downlink exposure in urban street canyons (such as Helsinki and New York City), demonstrating that distributed and cell-free massive MIMO architectures yield highly uniform exposure profiles compared to conventional collocated installations. Furthermore, the group integrates live base station telemetry—specifically actual Equivalent Isotropically Radiated Power (EIRP) counters recorded directly from operating massive MIMO antennas—to validate that actual human exposures are orders of magnitude below standard limits.Nagoya Institute of Technology (Japan)The Center for Biomedical Physics and Information Technology at the Nagoya Institute of Technology, directed by Akimasa Hirata, is a key academic hub for computational bioelectromagnetics. Hirata, who serves as the Chair of the ICNIRP Main Commission, has aligned the group’s computational activities with global policy-making.The group is recognized for its work in identifying and quantifying model variability and uncertainty in computational dosimetry. By developing voxel-based anatomical models and computational thermal engines, they have established quantitative relationships between local SAR, peak spatial-average APD ($psS_{ab}$), and temperature rises across diverse age groups and populations. A central contribution is the evaluation of the steady-state thermal "heating factor," defined as the local temperature rise normalized to the absorbed power or SAR, in sensitive tissues like the brain, eye lens, and skin.From a computational engineering standpoint, the Nagoya group has pioneered numerical techniques to mitigate voxelization and "staircasing" artifacts that naturally degrade the accuracy of FDTD solvers at tissue interfaces. Because low-frequency coupling is highly reactive, the Utah group simulated exposure at a higher virtual frequency ($f'$ up to $10\text{ MHz}$) and scaled the internal fields back to $60\text{ Hz}$, reducing the required iteration count in the time-domain solver by over five orders of magnitude.In clinical oncology, ionizing radiation dosimetry is managed by agencies such as the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency (ARPANSA) and its Australian Clinical Dosimetry Service (ACDS). ARPANSA designs international auditing protocols using physical phantom models and film-based dosimetry. When exposed to radiotherapy beams, specialized film inside human phantoms changes color, allowing clinical medical physicists to audit dose placement.While ARPANSA provides simple low-power exclusion calculators for body-worn equipment , their clinical auditing operates on physical measurements , contrasting with the purely numerical, non-ionizing electromagnetic solvers developed by the academic groups profiled above.Computational Paradigms in Dynamic RF Exposure AssessmentThe execution of computational dosimetry has evolved from isolated, single-physics solvers to integrated, multi-scale computational pipelines. By computing the deterministic multipath propagation across a geographic mesh, ray-tracing solvers capture the complex field vectors at any coordinate. These fields are mapped onto a virtual "Huygens' box" enclosing a high-resolution anatomical phantom. The FDTD solver is then executed locally inside the box, reducing computational overhead and enabling the simulation of realistic, dynamic exposure along a pedestrian path with speed improvements of up to 64-fold.Sionna-Based Ray-Tracing: High-Fidelity Phase ConstraintsNVIDIA Sionna is a GPU-accelerated physical-layer communications simulator that has been adapted by researchers to model complex propagation channels. Unlike classical statistical models, Sionna’s ray-tracer calculates exact multi-path trajectories, phase vectors, and polarizations.However, when adapting communications-centric ray-tracing engines like Sionna for bio-dosimetry, researchers must account for physical limitations noted in scattering models. For instance, in Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) or high-frequency scattering scenarios, received signals exhibit quadratic phase variations in the azimuth dimension as the transceiver moves.If the target scattering surface area is small (such as a $1\text{ m}^2$ plane) or the distance is high (such as $500\text{ m}$), the low number of intersecting rays results in spatial phase noise and distance-dependent discrepancies. Resolving this noise requires reducing the source-to-target distance or executing extensive path-averaging algorithms, which are critical considerations when adapting communications engines for high-precision human dosimetry.Machine Learning, PINNs, and Exposure Digital TwinsThe integration of artificial intelligence has shifted computational dosimetry from post-hoc analysis to real-time, predictive workflows. Rather than relying solely on full-wave FDTD simulations, which can take hours to complete, machine learning algorithms are trained on extensive prior databases to act as ultra-fast surrogate solvers.A key development is the use of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). This enables continuous virtual dosimetry across populations in high-density base station environments.Technical Synthesis and ConclusionsThe discipline of academic computational dosimetry has transitioned from static, decoupled evaluations of single devices to highly integrated, dynamic multi-physics frameworks. This transition is driven by the need to evaluate compliance against updated standards (ICNIRP 2020 / IEEE ICES 2019) under realistic operational conditions, such as massive MIMO beamforming and user mobility.Historically, safety assessments relied on rigid, worst-case conservative assumptions, such as placing a handset directly against a static, homogeneous physical phantom (like SAM) transmitting at continuous maximum power. Modern academic research, led by Tier 1 and Tier 2 institutions, has demonstrated that such static paradigms do not reflect real-world exposure.The integration of hybrid workflows—coupling GPU-accelerated propagation engines (e.g., Sionna, custom ray-tracers) with localized FDTD voxel solvers and live base station telemetry—allows for the simulation of the "actual" cumulative exposure of individuals moving through complex urban landscapes.Simultaneously, the incorporation of tissue anisotropy (e.g., muscle fiber alignment in the Alvar model) and stochastic uncertainty propagation (via the Stochastic Collocation Method) has improved the biological fidelity of these models. This ensures that calculated internal electric fields, localized temperature transients, and absorbed power densities are accurate representations of human physiology.The future of computational dosimetry lies in the maturation of real-time "exposure digital twins". Driven by Physics-Informed Neural Networks and synthetic anatomical generators, these systems can provide real-time, personalized, and clinically validated dosimetry, supporting both regulatory compliance and environmental safety monitoring.
